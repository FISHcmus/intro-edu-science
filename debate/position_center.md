# Academic Integrity and AI Fraud in EdTech Education: A Center-Pragmatist Policy Analysis

**Position: Center / Pragmatic Technocrat**
*Brookings/OECD Style — Evidence-Based, Institutionally Grounded, Internationally Comparative*

---

## Executive Summary

The emergence of generative AI tools has created a genuine academic integrity crisis — not an imagined one, and not merely a symptom of structural injustice to be talked away. University of Sheffield documented a 1,433% increase in confirmed AI misconduct cases in a single academic year (6 cases in 2022-23 to 92 in 2023-24). Springer Nature retracted 2,923 papers in 2024. NeurIPS 2025 — the world's premier AI conference — had hallucinated citations in 51 accepted papers, detected by zero of the 3+ peer reviewers assigned to each. In Vietnam, a Springer 2024 list-experiment study of 1,386 undergraduates found that actual AI cheating rates are approximately three times higher than self-reported rates.

The center position is this: the problem is real, the harms are measurable, and both extremes — the left's tendency to dissolve the problem into structural critique, and the right's tendency to treat it as a market-solvable detection problem — are inadequate responses. What is needed is a three-tier policy framework that combines institutional redesign, national regulatory clarity, and international coordination, calibrated for the specific constraints of different national contexts including Vietnam.

---

## 1. Evidence Synthesis: What the Research Actually Shows

### 1.1 Scale of the Problem

The data on AI-assisted academic dishonesty reveals a crisis of structural, not marginal, proportions:

- **Detection rates are catastrophically low relative to actual incidence.** The Springer 2024 Vietnamese study (Nguyen et al., doi:10.1007/s10639-024-12495-4) found a 3:1 ratio of actual AI cheating to self-reported AI cheating using validated indirect measurement (list experiment methodology). Applied to Vietnam's 2.1 million undergraduates, this implies a systemic credential gap.
- **UK institutional data confirms explosive growth.** Guardian FOI data shows nearly 7,000 confirmed AI cheating cases across UK universities in 2023-24 (5.1 per 1,000 students). Sheffield's 15x year-on-year increase is the trend, not an outlier.
- **Research integrity is compromised at elite venues.** Hindawi/Wiley retracted 8,000+ papers in 2023 — approximately 80% of all global retractions that year — at a financial cost of $35-40 million. NeurIPS 2025 reported 100+ hallucinated citations in 51 accepted papers, caught by zero peer reviewers out of 4,841 scanned. Springer Nature's 2,923 retractions in 2024 represent a knowledge contamination event with cascading downstream effects.
- **Detection tools are significantly less reliable than marketed.** Turnitin claims 98% accuracy; Washington Post independent testing found approximately 50%. GPTZero claims a 0.24% false positive rate; Liang et al. (Stanford, 2023) measured a 61.3% false positive rate on TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers. For Vietnamese students writing in English, these detection tools constitute a discriminatory intervention dressed as an integrity measure.

### 1.2 What Current Responses Are Getting Wrong

Most institutional responses in 2022-2024 fell into one of two failure modes:

**Over-enforcement without due process:** Texas A&M (May 2023) initially failed an entire class based on AI detector output before retracting the decision. CUHK (Hong Kong, 2023) implemented expulsion-level penalties. UK's Office of the Independent Adjudicator (OIA) documented multiple cases of students wrongly accused via AI detection and successfully appealing. These cases produced chilling effects, not integrity culture.

**Under-enforcement through policy vacuum:** Vietnam's primary relevant instrument — Nghị định 109/2022/NĐ-CP Điều 20 — defines academic integrity categories without any AI-specific provisions. QĐ 1705/QĐ-TTg (October 2024) promotes AI integration in education without a single mention of academic integrity safeguards. Two policies pointing in opposite directions without a bridge provision.

The evidence suggests that neither aggressive detection-first approaches nor passive policy vacuums are effective. What works — as Bretag's 5-element framework established empirically from analysis of 40 Australian university policies in 2011 — is a proactive, values-based, institutionally supported approach that combines clear policy, educational support, and shared responsibility.

## 2. Policy Landscape: What Works, What Does Not

### 2.1 UNESCO 2021/2023 — Directionally Correct, Implementation-Weak

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) establishes the normative baseline: human rights protection, non-discrimination, transparency, accountability. The 2023 Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research is operationally more useful — it explicitly recommends assessment redesign over detection-first responses, positions AI literacy as a universal educational right, and flags AI detection tool bias as a specific concern.

What UNESCO gets right: the systemic framing. AI academic integrity cannot be solved institution by institution or detection tool by detection tool. It requires a governance architecture that reaches from classroom assessment design to international publishing standards.

What UNESCO gets wrong: voluntary recommendations without implementation pathways. The 2023 guidance has been cited extensively in academic literature and policy discussions; its actual uptake in national regulatory frameworks outside the OECD core remains minimal. Vietnam's QĐ 1705 postdates the UNESCO guidance and ignores it entirely on the integrity dimension.

### 2.2 EU AI Act (2024) — Useful Regulatory Infrastructure, Wrong Problem Framing

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classifies AI systems used in "education and vocational training" as high-risk under Annex III where they determine access or outcomes. Article 4 mandates AI literacy. Article 50 requires transparency disclosure for AI-generated content.

This is the most consequential global AI governance instrument. For academic integrity specifically:

- Article 10 (bias in training data) creates a compliance concern for AI detection tools deployed in EU-adjacent contexts — Turnitin's documented ESL bias and GPTZero's 61.3% false positive rate on TOEFL essays would likely not survive a formal Article 10 audit.
- Article 4 provides regulatory backing for mandatory AI literacy in educational curricula — this is actionable for national policy.
- The high-risk classification framework does not cleanly address generative AI used by students to produce academic work — the Act regulates AI systems deployed by institutions, not AI tools used by students.

The EU AI Act is useful scaffolding but not purpose-built for academic integrity. It needs to be supplemented by sector-specific guidance from education bodies.

### 2.3 ICAI Framework — Normatively Strong, Operationally Incomplete

The International Center for Academic Integrity's 6 core values (Honesty, Trust, Fairness, Respect, Responsibility, Courage) provide the normative architecture that all other frameworks operationalize. McCabe's empirical finding — that honor code cultures produce measurably better long-term integrity outcomes than enforcement-only cultures — is the most important single piece of evidence in the academic integrity literature and is consistently confirmed across national contexts.

What ICAI gets right: framing integrity as a positive institutional culture investment, not a policing problem. The Responsibility value is particularly important — it explicitly distributes accountability across institutions, faculty, and students rather than concentrating it on individual students who cheat in AI-vulnerable assessment environments.

What ICAI leaves underspecified: operational guidance for the AI era. The 6 values were articulated before generative AI; their application to AI-assisted academic work requires the additional frameworks developed by Eaton (postplagiarism, 2023), Perkins (AIAS 5-tier framework, 2023), and Bretag (5-element policy design, 2011).

### 2.4 What Actually Works: The Evidence-Based Short List

From the empirical record, the following interventions have documented effectiveness:

1. **Process-based assessment** (University of Sydney Learning Futures framework, Stanford 2023 policy update, Chartered College of Teaching UK 2023 guidance): Reduces AI substitution opportunity structurally rather than relying on post-hoc detection. Aligns with QĐ 1705's competency-based education mandate.
2. **Oral defense / spot viva** (University of Western Ontario, UC San Diego, Macquarie Law School): Authentication of the person, not the product. No false positive rate with competent examiners. Scalable via 10-20% random sampling.
3. **The AIAS 5-tier framework** (Perkins, British University Vietnam, 2023): Adopted by hundreds of institutions globally, translated into 30+ languages, recognized by TEQSA (Australia). BUV's pilot — conducted in Vietnam — showed measurable reduction in AI-related misconduct. This is the most operationally specific framework available and has the highest direct relevance to Vietnamese HE.
4. **Honor code culture investment** (McCabe, Rutgers/ICAI): Long-term integrity outcomes are superior in honor-code cultures. Culture is upstream of all technical interventions.
5. **Bretag's 5-element policy audit** (2011, applied to AI contexts 2023-24): Access, Approach, Responsibility, Detail, Support. No AI integrity policy that fails on three of these five elements has been shown to be effective. Vietnam's current Decree 109 fails on at least three.

## 3. What the Center Accepts from the Left

The critical sociology tradition represented by Neil Selwyn (Monash) and Wayne Holmes (UCL/UNESCO) raises concerns that are empirically grounded and should not be dismissed:

**The detection tool bias problem is real and severe.** Liang et al.'s (Stanford, 2023) finding of a 61.3% false positive rate for non-native English speakers on GPTZero is not a marginal caveat — it is a finding that structurally disqualifies AI detection tools from sole-evidence use in disciplinary proceedings for ESL student populations. Vietnamese students writing in English are at specific, documented risk from tools marketed with a claimed 0.24% false positive rate. This is not ideology; it is statistics.

**The structural incentives that produce cheating are real.** McCabe's foundational research is clear: "publish or perish" pressures for researchers and credential inflation pressures for students create predictable rational incentives toward fraud. The Hindawi/Wiley case — 8,000+ retractions driven primarily by guest editors from developing countries working under systems where journal publications determine career progression — is a direct illustration of Selwyn's structural critique operating at industrial scale.

**Assessment design is partly responsible for enabling AI fraud.** If an assignment can be answered adequately by ChatGPT in two minutes, the institution that designed that assignment shares responsibility for the fraud it invites. This is not victim-blaming; it is institutional responsibility. Bretag's framework makes this explicit: the Responsibility element assigns shared accountability to institutions and faculty for assessment design that creates integrity vulnerabilities.

**Surveillance-based detection without educational support is counterproductive.** Holmes's critique of "surveillance AI" in education is not abstract — the documented pattern of universities disabling Turnitin AI Detection (Vanderbilt, Michigan State, Northwestern, UT Austin) after false positive controversies demonstrates that detection-first responses can damage the institutional trust that integrity cultures require.

## 4. What the Center Accepts from the Right

The pro-innovation, market-oriented perspective raises points that are also empirically grounded:

**AI tools genuinely improve learning for many students when used appropriately.** Luckin's argument in *Machine Learning and Human Intelligence* (UCL, 2018) — that AI can serve as a democratizing intelligence amplifier, extending the zone of proximal development beyond what individual teachers can provide — is not merely optimistic rhetoric. Khanmigo's Socratic design demonstrates that integrity-by-design is technically feasible. Students with disabilities, ESL learners, and students in under-resourced educational contexts have genuine access benefits from well-designed AI learning tools.

**Prohibition without support produces underground adoption.** The Vietnamese Springer 2024 study's finding that actual AI cheating is 3x self-reported is, in part, a concealment problem created by ambiguous policy environments. When students are uncertain whether AI use is permitted, they default to concealment. Clearer permissive frameworks — such as the AIAS 5-tier model that explicitly defines which AI uses are appropriate for which assessment levels — reduce concealment incentives.

**Innovation in EdTech creates genuine integrity-supporting capabilities.** Coursera's AI-based Viva Exam (June 2024), Khan Academy Khanmigo's conversation transparency (all student-AI dialogue visible to educators), and Google Workspace revision history as process documentation infrastructure are genuine technological contributions to the integrity challenge. These should be encouraged, not dismissed as commercial interests disguised as pedagogy.

**Blanket AI prohibition is neither enforceable nor educationally defensible.** The experience of institutions that attempted blanket prohibition in 2022-2023 — before policy frameworks matured — produced the Texas A&M false-positive crisis and the CUHK expulsion controversy, not integrity culture. The evidence strongly supports contextual regulation over prohibition.

## 5. What the Center Rejects from Both Sides

### 5.1 What the Left Gets Wrong

**Dissolving real harms into structural critique is analytically irresponsible.** The claim that AI cheating is "primarily a symptom of structural inequality" — while containing a kernel of truth — cannot be used to minimize the 8,000 retracted Hindawi papers, the 51 NeurIPS papers with hallucinated citations, or the 3x concealment rate among Vietnamese undergraduates. Students in disadvantaged positions who are encouraged by progressive critics to see AI fraud as structural resistance are not being helped — they are being denied the genuine competency development their credentials will claim they have.

**The false positive concern does not invalidate all enforcement.** The 61.3% GPTZero false positive rate on TOEFL essays is a powerful argument against using AI detection as sole evidence. It is not an argument against any enforcement. The Aryan Anand (Lehigh, 2024) and Haishan Yang (Minnesota, 2024-2026) cases involved students who unambiguously submitted AI-generated work as their own — and institutional responses that appropriately documented and penalized these violations were justified.

**Equity concerns should produce better tools and fairer procedures, not no enforcement.** The center position is: (1) AI detection tools with documented ESL bias should not be used as sole evidence; (2) All detection results should trigger a conversation with the student before any penalty; (3) Assessment redesign should reduce detection dependence. This is not the same as accepting that all integrity enforcement is discriminatory.

### 5.2 What the Right Gets Wrong

**Technology does not solve the collective action problem in academic integrity.** LLM watermarking — the most technically sophisticated proposed solution — requires simultaneous adoption by all major LLM providers to be effective. Scott Aaronson built a functional watermarking system for OpenAI; OpenAI chose not to deploy it, citing competitive disadvantage if watermarking only applied to their outputs. This is a governance problem, not a technology problem. Market incentives produce this outcome. Only coordinated regulation — analogous to the EU AI Act's transparency requirements — can address it.

**The arms race between detection and evasion tools has no market equilibrium.** Tools like Undetectable.ai, QuillBot, and HIX.AI are specifically trained to defeat GPTZero and Turnitin AI Detection. The commercial market produces both the fraud-enabling tools and the detection tools and the evasion tools simultaneously. This is not a market failure to be solved by innovation; it is a structural feature of uncoordinated markets. Only governance can break the cycle.

**Assessment redesign cannot be delegated entirely to individual faculty entrepreneurship.** Faculty who implement process-based assessment, oral defense, and staged submissions in isolation bear enormous workload costs. Sustainable implementation requires institutional investment, standardized rubrics, LMS integration, and professional development — none of which markets provide automatically.

## 6. Proposed 3-Tier Policy Framework

### Tier 1: Institutional Level

**Assessment Redesign (Mandatory, not Aspirational)**

Institutions should adopt the AIAS 5-tier framework as the standard for assignment design documentation. Every assignment should specify, at creation, which AIAS level applies (from Level 1: No AI to Level 5: Full AI Integration). This is not onerous — it is a single additional field in assignment templates. The British University Vietnam pilot demonstrates feasibility in a Vietnamese HE context.

For high-stakes summative assessments, institutions should implement at minimum two of the following three authentication mechanisms: (a) process documentation (Google Docs revision history, staged draft submission with timestamps); (b) oral follow-up (spot viva at 10-20% random sampling rate); (c) in-person timed component.

**Detection as Advisory, Not Sole Evidence**

AI detection tools (Turnitin AI, GPTZero, Copyleaks) may be used as one layer of a multi-signal investigation. No grade penalty shall be applied based on detector output alone. The detection result triggers a procedural review — conversation with student, review of process evidence — before any formal misconduct finding.

This is not radical; it mirrors best-practice jurisprudence. AI detector output has the evidentiary status of a preliminary indicator, not a verdict.

**AI Literacy as Core Curriculum (Bretag "Support" Element)**

All students should receive AI literacy instruction at program entry covering: (a) what generative AI tools do and cannot do; (b) institution-specific AI use policies for their program; (c) attribution and disclosure requirements; (d) consequences of undisclosed AI use. This is a 2-hour module, not a semester course. Its absence is the single cheapest-to-fix gap in most institutional AI integrity responses.

**Culture Investment (McCabe "Honor Code" Element)**

Institutions should audit their current policies against Bretag's 5 elements. In most cases, the Access and Support elements are the lowest-scoring: students cannot find the policy, and no support is provided for compliant AI use. These are administrative failures, not cultural ones, and are fixable with modest investment.

### Tier 2: National Level

**Vietnam-Specific: Bridge the Decree 109 / QĐ 1705 Gap**

The structural policy problem in Vietnam is that Nghị định 109/2022/NĐ-CP defines academic integrity categories (without AI) and QĐ 1705/QĐ-TTg promotes AI adoption (without integrity safeguards). A MOET Ministerial Circular is the appropriate instrument to bridge this gap without requiring legislative revision. The circular should:

1. Define AI-assisted academic misconduct within the existing Nghị định 109 categories, using Eaton's postplagiarism framework's responsibility principle (Tenet 4: you can outsource writing to AI but not responsibility) as the definitional foundation.
2. Mandate that Vietnamese HEIs adopt an AI use policy framework at institution level by a specified date, with Bretag's 5-element audit as the compliance standard.
3. Define minimum disclosure requirements: all academic submissions must include an AI use statement (either "AI was not used" or "AI was used as follows: [specification]").
4. Prohibit use of AI detection tool output as sole evidence in disciplinary proceedings, consistent with Turnitin's own usage guidelines.
5. Establish a national AI integrity monitoring mechanism — annual aggregated statistics on reported cases, analogous to UK FOI data — to build an evidence base for policy iteration.

**All National Systems: Minimum Standards for AI Detection Tools in Education**

Governments should require that AI detection tools deployed in educational enforcement contexts undergo bias audits against demographically representative student writing samples before deployment. This mirrors existing requirements for hiring AI tools under EU AI Act Article 10. The ESL false positive problem is not an ESL problem — it is a tool quality problem that produces discriminatory enforcement outcomes in any multilingual educational context.

**All National Systems: Align AI Literacy Requirements with EU AI Act Article 4**

The EU AI Act's AI literacy mandate provides a model that non-EU countries can adopt by reference. Vietnam's QĐ 127/QĐ-TTg (National AI Strategy) already references AI competency development; a supplementary provision linking this to UNESCO's AI literacy standards for education would require minimal regulatory effort.

### Tier 3: International Level

**LLM Watermarking Coordination**

Scott Aaronson's OpenAI experience demonstrates that voluntary market solutions to watermarking will not emerge. The appropriate mechanism is an international standards body (ISO, IEEE, or a UNESCO-convened technical group) establishing watermarking as a mandatory technical specification for LLMs deployed in educational contexts. This does not require every general-purpose LLM to watermark — it requires that LLMs marketed for educational use or integrated into educational platforms do so. This is analogous to how food safety labeling works: voluntary for internal consumption, mandatory for commercial distribution to protected populations.

**Publisher-Side Citation Verification Mandate**

The NeurIPS 2025 hallucinated citation crisis and the Hindawi/Wiley retraction catastrophe are both addressable through submission-stage citation verification. COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics), CrossRef, and major publisher groups should establish mandatory DOI verification at manuscript submission as a standard requirement. The technology exists — Elicit and Consensus already advertise grounded citation databases; submission platforms like OpenReview could integrate CrossRef validation at no significant technical cost. What is missing is industry-wide coordination to require it.

**International Academic Integrity Data Sharing**

The Vietnamese Springer 2024 study's list-experiment methodology represents best practice for measuring actual (rather than self-reported) AI fraud prevalence. This methodology should be adopted internationally as a standard for periodic national assessment of academic integrity conditions. UNESCO is the appropriate convening body. Without comparable cross-national data, policy evaluation remains impressionistic.

## 7. Vietnam-Specific Recommendations

Vietnam faces a distinctive combination of factors that make generic international guidance insufficient:

**The scale problem is real and untracked.** The Springer 2024 study's 3x concealment ratio, applied to 2.1 million undergraduates, suggests a structural integrity problem that no institutional response has yet measured systematically. Vietnam has no FOI mechanism equivalent to the UK system that produced Sheffield's data. Policy without data is guesswork.

**Detection tools are particularly inappropriate for Vietnamese HE contexts.** Vietnamese students writing in English face the same ESL false positive risk documented by Liang et al. (Stanford 2023) — potentially higher, given that most Vietnamese students' English writing development occurs later and with more constrained exposure than TOEFL-exam populations. Deploying Turnitin AI or GPTZero as enforcement tools against Vietnamese students writing in English would reproduce the Texas A&M failure at national scale.

**The AIAS framework has local evidence behind it.** Mike Perkins's work at British University Vietnam (BUV, Hanoi) is the only documented empirical study of an AI integrity intervention conducted within a Vietnamese educational institution. The BUV AIAS pilot showed measurable reduction in GenAI-related misconduct. This local evidence base should be used to advocate for national AIAS adoption rather than importing frameworks developed for US or Australian contexts without Vietnamese validation.

**QĐ 1705's competency-based education mandate is the policy lever.** The strategy's explicit commitment to shifting from knowledge transmission to genuine competency development (phát triển năng lực) is the strongest argument for process-based assessment at the national level. Assessment reform that documents genuine competency development — staged submissions, oral defense, portfolio evidence — simultaneously fulfills QĐ 1705's stated objective and addresses AI substitution risk. These are not competing demands; they are the same demand expressed in different vocabularies.

**"Publish or perish" pressures in Vietnamese research amplify the problem.** The Hindawi/Wiley retraction wave involved significant numbers of authors from developing-country institutions — including Vietnamese researchers — working in systems where journal publication counts determine promotion. Vietnam's university autonomy expansion (tự chủ đại học) has introduced performance management frameworks that can create these incentives domestically. Research integrity policy must engage with promotion criteria, not just submission policies.

**The HCMUFA case (2023) demonstrates that existing law can cover AI fraud — but also demonstrates the inadequacy of ad-hoc enforcement without explicit policy.** The student received a 50% deduction — a lighter sanction than most regional peers (CUHK imposed expulsion; NTU imposed zero marks). More importantly, the case occurred without a written AI policy in place, raising due process concerns. Fair enforcement requires prior written notice of prohibited conduct. Vietnam's existing administrative law principles support this requirement; the practical gap is institutional AI policy development.

## 8. Red Lines: Non-Negotiables Regardless of Ideology

These positions are not subject to ideological negotiation because they follow directly from the empirical evidence and from foundational ethical principles:

**Red Line 1: No grade penalty based solely on AI detector output.**
The empirical case for this is settled. Turnitin's independent-tested accuracy is approximately 50%; GPTZero's false positive rate on ESL populations is 61.3%. Using these tools as sole evidence is equivalent to flipping a biased coin and calling the outcome a disciplinary finding. Every affected student has a right to a process that includes examination of additional evidence before any adverse determination.

**Red Line 2: No AI policy without prior publication and AI literacy education.**
Students are entitled to fair notice of prohibited conduct before enforcement. The Texas A&M case, the UK OIA false positive appeals, and the HCMUFA case all share a common feature: enforcement preceded clear policy communication. This is not a technicality — it is a fundamental due process requirement that applies as much in Vietnamese administrative law as in common law jurisdictions.

**Red Line 3: Detection tools deployed in educational enforcement must undergo documented bias audits against the relevant student population.**
This is not a demand for perfect tools; it is a demand for honest tools. A tool that produces 61.3% false positives against a target population and is marketed with a 0.24% false positive claim is not an imperfect tool — it is a misleading commercial claim that creates unjust outcomes. Institutions and governments that deploy these tools without independent validation bear institutional responsibility for the discrimination that results.

**Red Line 4: The integrity of the scholarly record is a collective public good that cannot be left to individual institutional self-regulation.**
The Hindawi/Wiley retraction wave and the NeurIPS hallucinated citations case demonstrate that institutional and market incentives are insufficient to protect research integrity. 8,000 retractions from a single publisher, and zero of 51 hallucinated-citation papers caught by expert peer reviewers, are not market failures waiting to be corrected — they are structural failures requiring coordinated governance. This is the strongest argument for the international tier of the proposed framework.

**Red Line 5: AI literacy is a prerequisite for AI integrity enforcement, not a nice-to-have supplement.**
Enforcing AI policies against students who have not been taught what those policies require violates the Bretag Support element, the ICAI Fairness value, and basic pedagogical ethics. Before detection comes education. Before education comes clear policy. The sequence is non-negotiable.

## Conclusion: The Reasonable Adult Position

The academic integrity and AI fraud debate has been captured by two failure modes. One side treats every concern about AI cheating as a disguised attempt to surveil marginalized students with biased tools. The other treats every question about detection tool accuracy as an attempt to enable fraud.

The reasonable adult position is that both of these framings are wrong because both are selective with the evidence. The evidence shows that AI academic fraud is real, widespread, and growing; that current detection tools are inadequate and biased; that assessment design is the primary preventive lever; that culture is upstream of all technical interventions; and that governance gaps — not technology gaps — are the binding constraint on effective policy.

Luckin is right that AI can be a powerful democratizing learning tool. Selwyn is right that surveillance-based detection without structural reform will fail and harm the vulnerable. Eaton is right that the postplagiarism paradigm requires new normative frameworks, not just better enforcement of old ones. Perkins is right that the AIAS framework provides the most operationally specific path from principles to practice. And McCabe is right that honor code culture is the only thing that has been shown, empirically, to produce durable integrity outcomes.

The center position synthesizes these insights rather than choosing between them. It insists on measuring the problem honestly, regulating the tools fairly, redesigning the assessments structurally, building the culture deliberately, and coordinating governance internationally. None of this is ideologically remarkable. All of it is currently not being done at scale.

For Vietnam specifically: the gap between QĐ 1705's ambition and Decree 109's silence on AI is not an academic technicality. It is the policy vacuum in which 3x-the-acknowledged rate of AI fraud is occurring, untracked, in the education system that is supposed to be building the competencies on which the nation's development strategy depends. The ministerial circular proposed in Tier 2 is the minimum viable response. It is achievable, it is evidenced, and there is no principled argument — from any position on the ideological spectrum — for continuing to not do it.
