# Meta-Review: Academic Integrity and AI Fraud in EdTech Research Materials

**Reviewer role:** Senior research director, critical meta-review  
**Date:** 12/4/2026  
**Materials reviewed:** Research report (52 items), 6 position papers, debate transcript (4 rounds), final synthesis report, assignment submission

---

## 1. Research Gaps

### 1.1 Geographic Blind Spots

The research is heavily skewed toward the Anglophone world (US, UK, Australia) with a thin Vietnam layer and almost nothing else:

- **China** is mentioned only in passing (CUHK expulsion policy, Cyberspace Administration labeling rules). China is the world's largest higher education system and has its own massive AI fraud problem, its own detection ecosystem, and its own regulatory approach. The absence of any Chinese-language academic integrity research (e.g., work by Fengchun Miao at UNESCO Beijing, or domestic Chinese studies on AI fraud prevalence) is a significant gap.
- **India** is entirely absent. India has 40+ million students in higher education, a massive EdTech sector (BYJU's, Unacademy), and documented AI fraud problems in competitive exam contexts (UPSC, IIT-JEE coaching). The Indian context would test many of the report's assumptions about credential premium and detection tool deployment.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa** is absent. The research's recommendations about oral defense and process-based assessment assume institutional resources (TA availability, internet access for Google Docs) that may not hold in resource-constrained African universities.
- **Latin America** is absent. Brazil and Mexico have significant EdTech adoption and different academic culture norms around citation and collaboration.
- **Middle East / Gulf States** are absent. The UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar are heavy EdTech investors with distinct academic integrity challenges in branch campus contexts.
- **Southeast Asia beyond Vietnam** gets cursory treatment. Singapore (NUS, NTU) is mentioned for policy; Indonesia, Philippines, Thailand, and Malaysia are absent despite having large student populations and growing AI adoption.

### 1.2 Disciplinary Gaps

The research report's case studies are overwhelmingly from STEM, CS, and social sciences. Missing disciplines:

- **Medicine and health sciences**: The Neurosurgical Review retraction wave is mentioned, but there is no systematic treatment of AI fraud in clinical education, medical board preparation, or nursing education. These are the highest-stakes professional credentialing contexts where fraud has direct patient safety consequences. The GMC (UK General Medical Council) and NBME (US National Board of Medical Examiners) have published responses to AI fraud that are not cited.
- **Law**: The report mentions AI passing bar exams but does not examine AI fraud in legal education specifically. Law school honor codes, legal writing pedagogy, and the ABA's (American Bar Association) emerging guidance on AI in law schools are absent.
- **Creative arts and design**: The HCMUFA case is the only arts example. How does AI fraud manifest in studio art, architecture, music composition, or film? These disciplines face fundamentally different AI challenges (image generation, music generation) that text-focused detection tools cannot address.
- **K-12 education**: The entire research corpus treats higher education only. AI fraud in secondary schools, particularly in high-stakes contexts like Vietnam's THPT national exam or the UK's A-levels, is a major policy concern that is completely absent.
- **Vocational and technical education**: TVET (technical and vocational education and training) is absent. Assessment integrity for practical skills certifications faces different challenges than academic essay assessment.

### 1.3 Temporal Gaps

- **Historical precedents**: The research treats AI fraud as if it emerged from a vacuum in November 2022. There is no engagement with the history of technological disruption in academic integrity: the introduction of the photocopier, the internet, Wikipedia, essay mills, Chegg, and how institutions adapted to each. This historical context would provide useful lessons about the pace and nature of institutional adaptation.
- **Pre-ChatGPT AI tools**: GPT-2 (2019) and GPT-3 (2020) were already used for academic fraud before ChatGPT. The Jasper.ai and Copy.ai era of 2021-2022 is not discussed. The framing of November 2022 as the origin point is convenient but historically inaccurate.

### 1.4 Missing Evidence and Counterevidence

- **Studies showing AI detection tools working well**: The research presents Liang et al. (2023) as definitive but does not engage with subsequent work that may show improved detection accuracy. Weber-Wulff et al. (2023) is cited in passing by the Technologist but not deeply analyzed. Are there more recent studies (2024-2025) showing detection tools improving? The absence of counterevidence weakens the argument even if the conclusion is correct.
- **Quantitative evidence on oral defense effectiveness**: Everyone endorses spot vivas, but the empirical evidence cited is thin -- University of Western Ontario (one institution, one year), Macquarie Law School (anecdotal reports of "improved engagement"). Where are the controlled studies comparing cheating rates under oral defense regimes vs. traditional assessment? The recommendation is being made on theoretical plausibility, not experimental evidence.
- **Cost-benefit analysis of assessment redesign**: The Economist claims spot vivas have positive NPV but provides no actual numbers beyond "10 hours per 300 students." What is the actual institutional cost of redesigning assessment at scale? What are the training costs? The opportunity costs of faculty time? The claim that assessment redesign is cheaper than detection tool subscriptions is asserted, not demonstrated.
- **Student self-report data on WHY they use AI**: The Springer 2024 Vietnamese study (N=1,386) measures prevalence but apparently not motivation. Why do Vietnamese students use AI fraudulently? Is it time pressure, lack of understanding of the material, financial stress, unclear policy, or perceived low risk? Without motivation data, the policy prescriptions are guessing at causes.

### 1.5 Missing Scholars and Sources

- **Deborah Weber-Wulff** (HTW Berlin) -- one of the most prolific researchers on AI detection tool evaluation. Her 2023 systematic testing of 14 AI detection tools is cited once in passing but deserves central treatment.
- **Irene Glendinning** (Coventry University) -- leading European researcher on academic integrity policy, particularly the EU-funded IPPHEAE project comparing integrity policies across 27 European countries.
- **Teddi Fishman** -- former director of ICAI, key figure in integrity policy development.
- **COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics)** guidelines on AI authorship -- mentioned once in the final report but their 2023 position statement on AI and authorship is directly relevant and not analyzed.
- **WAME (World Association of Medical Editors)** and **ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors)** statements on AI in medical publishing -- absent despite the research integrity focus.
- **Asian academic integrity researchers**: Where is Ting-Chia Hsu (Taiwan), Shivadas Sivasubramaniam (South Africa/UK), or Tomoko Ikegami (Japan)? The field is presented as entirely Western.

## 2. Debate Weaknesses

### 2.1 Weakest Position Papers

**The Right-Wing paper** is the weakest in its positive prescriptions. Its diagnosis (credential inflation, broken incentives) is strong and well-argued. But its solutions -- credential unbundling, market competition, voluntary industry standards -- are aspirational and lack a credible implementation pathway for Vietnam. The paper cites Google/Apple/IBM Skills First hiring as evidence, but these programs operate in the US tech sector, which is a fundamentally different labor market from Vietnam's public sector, teaching profession, or manufacturing economy. The LW and EC rebuttals on this point are devastating and not adequately answered. The paper's steelman of the opposition (Section 6) is actually good, but the response to it ("reserve mandatory intervention for professional licensing contexts") concedes most of the ground the left-wing position occupies.

**The Educator paper** is the weakest in engaging with practical constraints. It reads as an idealized vision of what assessment should look like, without adequate engagement with the reality of Vietnamese HE: class sizes of 200-400 students, faculty workloads that already exceed capacity, institutional cultures where the teacher's authority is not challenged, and limited TA infrastructure. The three-year faculty development program (Recommendation 6) is correct in principle but reads as if budget and political will are unlimited. The paper does not engage with what happens when assessment redesign fails or is poorly implemented.

### 2.2 Positions Not Steelmanned Properly

**The "do nothing" position** was never represented or steelmanned. There is a legitimate argument that the AI fraud "crisis" will self-correct as institutions adapt, as happened with previous technological disruptions (the internet, Wikipedia, essay mills). The argument would go: institutions have always adapted to new cheating technologies, the current panic is cyclical, and heavy-handed policy intervention now will produce unintended consequences that are worse than the problem. No one at the table makes this case, and no one engages with it.

**The surveillance/proctoring position** was universally rejected (and correctly so, in my view), but it was not steelmanned. Companies like Proctorio, ExamSoft, and Respondus have published data claiming their tools reduce cheating rates. The argument that continuous monitoring is justified for high-stakes professional licensing exams (medical boards, bar exams, pilot certification) is never seriously engaged with. The universal rejection conflates mass undergraduate surveillance with targeted professional certification monitoring.

**The AI company position** was absent entirely. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google have published responsible use policies, educational guidelines, and (in OpenAI's case) a stated position on watermarking. Meta has a specific open-source AI philosophy that directly bears on the open-source model proliferation problem. These are not neutral actors -- they have commercial interests -- but their perspectives contain substantive arguments about why watermarking was not deployed (competitive dynamics, user trust, accuracy degradation) that deserve engagement.

### 2.3 Missed Rebuttals in the Debate

- **Round 2**: No one challenges the framing of Liang et al. (2023) as the final word on detection accuracy. The study tested TOEFL essays, which are a specific genre of formulaic academic English. Are detection tools equally biased against Vietnamese students writing in Vietnamese? The Liang et al. finding is about English-medium detection of ESL writing; its applicability to Vietnamese-medium instruction is assumed but never tested. This is a major inferential leap that goes unquestioned.

- **Round 3**: The Economist's NPV calculation for spot vivas is never challenged on its assumptions. 10 hours per assessment cycle for 300 students assumes 10% sampling. But if students learn that only 10% are sampled, the deterrent effect depends on the perceived probability of being sampled. If students calculate that 90% chance of not being called for viva makes AI fraud still positive-EV, the deterrence argument collapses. No one raises this game theory problem.

- **Round 4**: LW's proposal to prohibit AI detectors trained on native-English corpora is accepted without pushback on its practical implications. What detection tools WOULD be permissible in Vietnamese proceedings? Are there detection tools trained on Vietnamese-language corpora? If not, the prohibition effectively bans all AI detection, which may or may not be the intention. This ambiguity is not resolved.

- **Throughout**: No one challenges the McCabe honor code finding (25-30% lower cheating rates) on its methodological limitations. McCabe studied US institutions. Honor code cultures at US universities (West Point, UVA, Rice) have specific institutional histories, selection effects (students who choose honor code schools may be different), and cultural contexts that may not transfer to Vietnamese universities. The finding is treated as universal when it may be culturally specific.

### 2.4 Strawmanning

The CUHK expulsion policy is used throughout as a strawman for "bad policy." But CUHK's actual policy was more nuanced than "expulsion for first offense" -- it included graduated penalties with expulsion as the maximum for severe cases. The debate treats it as a blanket zero-tolerance policy, which is a misrepresentation that weakens the overall analysis.

## 3. Logical Gaps in the Final Report

### 3.1 Overstated Consensus Claims

**Claim 1.2: "AI Fraud Is Primarily a Structural Problem, Not an Individual Moral Failure"** -- described as consensus. But the Right-Wing paper explicitly argues for "individual responsibility" within structural context, and the Economist's cost-benefit framing treats students as rational actors making individual choices. The consensus is that structural factors are important, not that they are "primary." The word "primarily" is doing heavy lifting that not all positions actually support.

**Claim 1.6: "LLM Watermarking Is the Correct Long-Term Technical Solution"** -- described as consensus. But the Technologist explicitly notes that watermarking cannot affect open-source models, and by 2028 capable open-source models will be ubiquitous. If watermarking only covers commercial API calls while students use local Llama variants, it is not a "solution" -- it is a partial mitigation that sophisticated users can trivially bypass. The consensus that watermarking is "correct" is actually consensus that it is "technically sound but may be practically irrelevant."

**Claim 1.8: "McCabe's Honor Code Finding Is the Most Important Single Datum"** -- this is stated as if all six positions agree on "most important." The Economist clearly treats the credential premium and Akerlof's lemons dynamic as more important. The Technologist treats the Liang et al. false positive finding as more important for immediate policy. The claim of unanimous agreement on "most important" is editorial overstatement.

### 3.2 Irreconcilable Differences That Are Not Actually Irreconcilable

**3.1: "What Education Is For"** -- The report frames LW's "education for human flourishing" vs. RW's "education for credential signaling" as irreconcilable. But most actual education systems serve both purposes simultaneously, and most educators accept both functions. The dichotomy is a philosophical abstraction that does not prevent practical policy agreement. The debate transcript itself shows that LW and RW converge on assessment redesign despite this "irreconcilable" difference -- which suggests it is reconcilable in practice even if not in theory.

**3.2: "Institutional Actors as Trustworthy vs. Captured"** -- This is presented as a deep philosophical divide, but in the debate both sides acknowledge that both market and state actors can fail. The actual disagreement is about which failure mode is more common in Vietnam's specific context -- a factual question that could in principle be investigated empirically.

### 3.3 The "7 Rejected Proposals" -- Overstatement

**Rejected Proposal 5: "Relying exclusively on voluntary student AI disclosure without structural verification"** -- the report says this was "rejected" but then notes it was "endorsed only as one element in a multi-layered system." That is not rejection -- that is conditional acceptance. The framing as "rejected" is misleading.

**Rejected Proposal 6: "Delegating academic integrity frameworks entirely to international EdTech vendors"** -- the word "entirely" makes this a strawman. No participant actually proposed this. Rejecting a proposal that no one made is not a finding.

### 3.4 Missing from the "Ultimate Solution Framework"

- **Accountability mechanisms**: The framework proposes many things institutions "should" do but never specifies what happens when they don't. Who enforces the MOET Circular? What are the consequences for non-compliance? Without enforcement design, the framework is aspirational.
- **Funding sources**: Assessment redesign, faculty professional development, oral defense infrastructure, AI literacy modules -- all cost money. The framework does not estimate costs or identify funding mechanisms. The QĐ 1705 "20% of state budget for education" is mentioned but never connected to specific budget lines.
- **Transition management**: The framework recommends ending detection-as-sole-evidence "immediately" while building assessment redesign over 1-3 years. What happens in the gap? If institutions stop using detection tools before assessment redesign is in place, fraud rates during the transition period may spike. No one addresses this transition risk.
- **Quality assurance for oral defense**: The framework assumes "competent examiners" but never addresses how to ensure examiner quality. Poorly trained examiners conducting vivas could produce worse outcomes than properly used detection tools. Faculty bias, favoritism, and inconsistency in oral assessment are well-documented problems that the framework ignores.
- **Vietnamese-language detection tools**: The entire detection discussion assumes English-medium tools. Are there Vietnamese-language AI detection tools? If Vietnam develops its own tools trained on Vietnamese student writing, does the ESL false positive argument apply? This technical question is policy-relevant and unanswered.

## 4. Missing Perspectives

### 4.1 Student Perspective

This is the most glaring omission. The entire corpus discusses students as objects of policy -- people to be assessed, detected, penalized, educated, or protected. At no point does any document present the authentic voice of students who have been:

- **Falsely accused** of AI fraud and experienced the psychological and academic consequences
- **Genuinely used AI** to learn more effectively and feel the policy environment is punitive rather than supportive
- **Cheated using AI** and could explain their actual decision-making process, motivations, and perceptions of risk
- **Reported other students** for AI fraud and experienced the social consequences

The LW paper mentions "student representation in academic integrity policy development" (Section V.6) but does not actually incorporate student perspectives. No survey data on student attitudes toward AI use policies in Vietnamese universities is cited. No qualitative research on student experiences with AI detection tools is referenced. The Springer 2024 Vietnamese study measures prevalence but apparently not student perspectives on fairness, motivation, or policy preferences.

### 4.2 Frontline Teacher in Vietnam

The Educator paper speaks from a "professional educator" perspective but reads like a Western education theorist, not a Vietnamese lecturer teaching 400 students with no TA support, unclear institutional policy, no training in AI-era assessment, and pressure from administrators to maintain enrollment numbers. The practical reality of implementing spot vivas or staged submissions in Vietnamese public universities -- where faculty are often underpaid, overloaded, and lacking institutional support -- is never honestly confronted.

### 4.3 Developing World Researcher

The research integrity discussion (Hindawi retractions, NeurIPS hallucinated citations) is framed from the perspective of journal editors and publishers. Missing is the perspective of a researcher at a resource-poor institution in Vietnam, Indonesia, or Nigeria who faces genuine publish-or-perish pressure, has limited access to expensive journals and research infrastructure, and may use AI tools not to commit fraud but to overcome language barriers in publishing in English-language journals. The line between "AI-assisted writing for language accessibility" and "AI-generated content fraud" is blurry in practice, and this ambiguity is not explored.

### 4.4 AI Company Perspective

OpenAI published its "Educator FAQ" in 2023 and has stated positions on watermarking, responsible use, and educational deployment. Anthropic has published its "Responsible Scaling Policy" and "Acceptable Use Policy." Google has SynthID for images and stated plans for text watermarking. These companies are not monolithic -- they have different approaches to the watermarking collective action problem. The Technologist paper describes the Aaronson/OpenAI watermarking episode but does not engage with the companies' own articulated positions on why they made the choices they made.

### 4.5 Legal/Due Process Perspective

The Haishan Yang case (University of Minnesota, 2024-2026) is the only legal case analyzed in depth. But there are emerging legal questions that no position paper addresses:

- **Burden of proof**: What standard of evidence should apply in AI misconduct proceedings? Preponderance of evidence? Clear and convincing? Beyond reasonable doubt? The choice of standard has enormous practical consequences and is never discussed.
- **Right to counsel/representation**: Do students have the right to legal representation in AI misconduct proceedings? In Vietnam, what are the administrative law protections?
- **Statute of limitations**: If AI fraud is discovered years after graduation, can degrees be revoked? What are the time limits?
- **Intellectual property**: Who owns AI-generated text submitted as coursework? The student? The AI company? The university? This has copyright implications that are unexplored.
- **Discrimination claims**: Could Vietnamese ESL students bring discrimination claims against universities that deploy biased detection tools? Under what legal framework?

## 5. What Should Be Researched Further

### 5.1 Immediate Priority Studies

1. **Vietnamese-language AI detection tool validation study**: Test GPTZero, Turnitin AI, and Copyleaks against a corpus of authentic Vietnamese student writing (both Vietnamese-medium and English-medium). Measure false positive rates by language, discipline, and student English proficiency level. This study would directly answer the question the entire debate assumes but cannot answer: how biased are these tools against Vietnamese students specifically?

2. **Vietnamese student motivation survey**: Conduct a mixed-methods study (survey + interviews) with Vietnamese university students asking WHY they use AI for academic work. Distinguish between: (a) time pressure, (b) lack of understanding of material, (c) unclear AI use policies, (d) perceived low detection risk, (e) belief that AI use is not cheating, (f) financial/employment pressure. The policy prescription depends entirely on which motivations dominate, and this data does not exist.

3. **Spot viva effectiveness RCT**: Design a randomized controlled trial comparing cheating rates (measured via list experiment methodology, as in the Springer 2024 study) across three conditions: (a) traditional assessment only, (b) traditional assessment + AI detection tool, (c) traditional assessment + 15% spot viva sampling. This would provide the experimental evidence that the entire debate lacks for its primary recommendation.

### 5.2 Medium-Term Studies

4. **Cost-benefit analysis of assessment redesign in Vietnamese HE**: Partner with 3-5 Vietnamese universities to implement AIAS framework and measure actual costs (faculty time, training, infrastructure) and outcomes (cheating rates, student learning outcomes, faculty satisfaction). Provide the economic data that the Economist's paper assumes but cannot cite.

5. **Cross-cultural validity of McCabe's honor code findings**: Replicate McCabe's honor code culture survey in Vietnamese, Chinese, and Indian university contexts. Test whether the 25-30% cheating rate reduction holds outside Anglo-American institutional cultures. If it does not, the "culture over enforcement" recommendation needs qualification.

6. **Longitudinal AIAS impact study at BUV**: Follow BUV's AIAS pilot over 3-5 years to determine whether the initial misconduct reduction persists, scales, and transfers to other Vietnamese institutions without Perkins-level implementation leadership.

### 5.3 Longer-Term Research

7. **Credential signal degradation study**: Conduct employer surveys in Vietnam measuring whether hiring managers are discounting Vietnamese university credentials relative to pre-AI baselines. Test Akerlof's lemons prediction empirically rather than theoretically.

8. **Open-source LLM proliferation impact study**: Track adoption of locally-run LLMs (Llama, Mistral, Qwen) among Vietnamese university students over 2026-2028. Measure whether detection tool effectiveness declines as the Technologist predicts.

## 6. Improvements to the Assignment Submission

The assignment submission (`week5/bai-tap-chuong3-liem-chinh-hoc-thuat-ai.md`) is well-structured and competently written. It is the strongest single document in the corpus for a course assignment. That said, the debate and research materials reveal several areas where it should be strengthened:

### 6.1 Add Acknowledgment of Limitations

The assignment presents its recommendations with high confidence but does not acknowledge:

- The lack of experimental evidence for spot viva effectiveness at scale
- The cultural specificity of McCabe's honor code findings (US-centric)
- The gap between AIAS framework endorsement and evidence of long-term effectiveness
- The transition problem: what happens between ending detection-as-sole-evidence and completing assessment redesign

A "Limitations" section (even 2-3 paragraphs) would significantly strengthen the academic rigor.

### 6.2 Strengthen the Vietnam-Specific Analysis

The assignment identifies the Decree 109/QĐ 1705 policy gap correctly. It should also:

- Discuss the practical constraints of implementing oral defense in Vietnamese public universities (class sizes, faculty workload, TA availability)
- Acknowledge that Vietnamese academic culture has specific features (hierarchical teacher-student relationships, exam-oriented assessment tradition, Confucian emphasis on effort) that affect both the problem and the proposed solutions
- Cite the HCMUFA case in more detail as the only documented Vietnamese AI fraud case, including its due process implications

### 6.3 Engage with Counterarguments

The assignment does not engage with the strongest counterarguments to its own position:

- "Assessment redesign is too expensive and slow for institutions facing the crisis now"
- "Oral defense introduces its own biases (verbal fluency, social anxiety, examiner prejudice)"
- "The AIAS framework is too new to have proven long-term effectiveness"
- "Vietnamese universities lack the institutional capacity to implement these recommendations"

Addressing these directly, even briefly, would demonstrate intellectual maturity.

### 6.4 Add Student Perspective

The assignment adopts the "EdTech researcher" perspective as instructed, but it would be strengthened by including:

- The perspective of Vietnamese students who face AI fraud accusations (fairness concerns)
- The perspective of honest students whose credentials are devalued by classmates' AI fraud
- Any available data on Vietnamese student attitudes toward AI use in education

### 6.5 Update with Debate Findings

The assignment was written before the debate materials. It should incorporate:

- The Economist's Akerlof/Spence analysis of credential signal degradation -- this provides a stronger theoretical foundation for why AI fraud matters economically
- The Technologist's argument about open-source LLM proliferation making detection obsolete by 2028 -- this strengthens the case for assessment redesign as the durable solution
- The left-wing critique of detection tools as systematic discrimination against ESL students -- the assignment mentions Liang et al. but could frame the equity argument more forcefully for the Vietnamese context

### 6.6 Tighten the Policy Recommendation

The assignment recommends a MOET Thong tu (Circular) but is vague about its contents. The final report's 15-point framework provides much more specific policy content. The assignment should incorporate at minimum:

- Specific definition of AI-assisted misconduct categories
- Mandatory AI use disclosure on all submissions
- Prohibition of detection tool output as sole disciplinary evidence
- National reporting requirements for AI misconduct cases
- Timeline for institutional compliance

## Summary Assessment

The research materials are substantially above average for an undergraduate course assignment. The research report covers 52 items with genuine depth. The six position papers represent distinct analytical traditions competently. The debate transcript produces real intellectual engagement, not performative disagreement. The final synthesis report is the strongest document -- its consensus/contested/irreconcilable structure is analytically sophisticated.

The fundamental weaknesses are:

1. **Echo chamber effect**: All six debate positions converge too quickly and too completely. Real intellectual disagreement was smoothed over rather than sharpened. A genuine adversarial debate would have produced more friction, more unresolved questions, and less tidy synthesis.

2. **Evidence thinness for the primary recommendation**: Everyone recommends assessment redesign and oral defense, but the experimental evidence base for this recommendation is remarkably thin. The recommendation is theoretically compelling but empirically underdemonstrated.

3. **Missing voices**: Students, frontline Vietnamese teachers, developing-world researchers, and AI companies are all absent. The debate is conducted entirely among policy elites talking about what should be done to/for people who are not in the room.

4. **Geographic and disciplinary narrowness**: The Anglophone bias and STEM/social science focus leave large parts of the global education landscape unexamined.

5. **Transition planning gap**: The framework says what should exist in the future but does not seriously address how to get there from the present, especially in resource-constrained Vietnamese institutions.

These are addressable weaknesses. The underlying analysis is sound. The materials would benefit most from: (a) adding primary data from Vietnamese students and teachers, (b) conducting a pilot study of the recommended interventions, and (c) engaging more honestly with the practical constraints of implementation.
