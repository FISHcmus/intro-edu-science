## A Structured Four-Round Debate

**Participants:**
- **RW** — Right-Wing Analyst (Heritage/AEI tradition, free-market solutions)

## ROUND 1 — Root Causes

**Question:** "Why does AI fraud happen? Is it individual moral failure, structural incentives, or technological inevitability?"

---

**RW:** AI fraud exploded after November 2022 not because a new generation became more dishonest, but because the marginal cost of fraud collapsed to zero. McCabe documented two-thirds of US college students cheating *before* AI. The incentive structure was already broken — credential inflation, bureaucratic assessment design, and a system that signals "what matters is the diploma, not the learning." AI didn't create a moral crisis. It exposed an incentive architecture that was already deformed. The fix is structural: break the credential monopoly, let employers verify competency directly, and let market competition sort institutions by actual graduate quality.

**LW:** RW is right on the diagnosis but stop there — "market mechanisms" as the cure is magical thinking. Selwyn named this a decade ago: the EdTech industry systematically sells technological solutions to problems that the same commercial logic manufactured. The credential economy is a *neoliberal* construction, not a neutral market phenomenon. Students in 300-person lecture halls, completing courses they take for accreditation rather than genuine interest, in a system that has replaced the educator-student relationship with a consumer-provider transaction — these students are not moral failures. They are actors in a system where the institution has already signaled that the credential is the product, not the learning. The moral responsibility is distributed, and institutions bear more of it than they admit.

**CT:** Both of you are partially right and both of you are stopping too early. The structural incentive analysis is correct — McCabe's two-thirds figure from before AI is the most important single datum in this debate. But "structural incentives" is not a policy prescription, it is a description. The question is what to do about those incentives now, with 2.1 million Vietnamese undergraduates in a system where actual AI fraud runs at 3x the self-reported rate, a QĐ 1705 that promotes AI adoption without a single integrity safeguard, and a Decree 109/2022 that defines academic integrity categories with no AI provision. The structural critique explains why we are here. It does not tell us what to do on Monday.

**EC:** There is a precise economic vocabulary for what is happening. Spence's signaling model predicts this exactly: when AI reduces the cost of producing a credential signal to near zero, the sorting function of that signal degrades. Akerlof's lemons model predicts the consequence: when buyers cannot distinguish genuine from fraudulent credentials, they rationally discount all credentials from affected systems. The Springer 2024 Vietnamese data — 3x actual versus self-reported fraud — is not a moral story, it is a market failure story. Adverse selection, negative externalities, and a prisoners' dilemma arms race between detection and evasion tools. Individual moral exhortation cannot correct structural market failures. Only institutional design can. The human capital destruction consequence is specific to Vietnam: in development economics (Hanushek and Woessmann 2012), cognitive skills — not merely credentials — drive long-run GDP growth. Vietnam is not in a position to absorb the human capital destruction of mass AI fraud.

**ED:** From a learning science perspective, the question "why does fraud happen" has one primary answer: because the assessments that are being "defrauded" were not measuring genuine learning in the first place. Gallant's central argument is the one that gets ignored most consistently: if an AI can trivially complete your assignment, your assignment was not testing a meaningful competency. Newton and Xiromeriti demonstrated empirically in 2023 that ChatGPT passes medical and law assessments. This is not primarily an AI story — it is an assessment design story. These assessments were already measuring the wrong things. AI has simply rendered the failure visible at scale. The moral failure framing is not only empirically wrong — it is institutionally convenient, because it redirects accountability away from the assessment designers and onto the students who responded rationally to the incentives those designers created.

**TE:** I will add a technical dimension the others have not fully addressed. AI fraud is partly technological inevitability — not because cheating is inevitable, but because the cost trajectory of fraud follows Moore's Law. Before November 2022, submitting high-quality AI-generated work required either genuine competency or expensive contract cheating (Newton 2018 estimated 15.7% market penetration at non-trivial direct cost). GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 drove the marginal cost of producing credible academic work to approximately zero overnight. This is an exogenous technological shock, not a gradual cultural shift. The institutional response needs to account for the fact that future models will be more capable, open-source models will proliferate beyond any regulatory reach, and multi-modal fraud in code, data analysis, and images is already arriving. Any policy built around detecting specific AI output signatures is fighting the last war.

### Rebuttals — Round 1

**LW challenges RW:** You invoke "market mechanisms" to solve credential inflation, but who manufactured credential inflation in the first place? It was the same market logic — employer over-reliance on degree signals, university expansion driven by tuition revenue rather than learning outcomes, government subsidization that created the monopoly you now want to dismantle. You cannot simultaneously diagnose credential inflation as a market-driven pathology and prescribe more market as the cure. The cure for a market failure is not a purer market — it is the structural reform that markets failed to produce on their own.

**RW challenges LW:** You accuse me of magical thinking on markets but your structural critique leads precisely nowhere when it comes to what Vietnamese institutions should do in the next academic year. Selwyn's critique of EdTech companies is sharp sociology. It is useless operational guidance. And your framing of detection tools as a "corporate ethics problem" obscures that the same Turnitin you condemn is also catching genuine academic fraud that is harming honest students. The 61.3% false positive rate is real and disqualifying for enforcement purposes, but the answer is better tools and better procedures, not abandoning enforcement entirely.

**EC challenges ED:** The assessment design argument is compelling and I accept its core premise. But it proves too much applied to research fraud. Hindawi's 8,000+ retracted papers, Springer Nature's 2,923 retractions in a single year, and NeurIPS 2025's 100+ hallucinated citations in 51 papers — these are not the result of poor assignment design. They are the result of publish-or-perish incentive structures that make fraud individually rational and collectively catastrophic. The assessment redesign prescription is correct for undergraduate education. It does not extend cleanly to the research integrity crisis, which requires different institutional mechanisms at the journal and conference level.

**ED challenges TE:** Your technological inevitability framing is partially correct but potentially paralyzing. Yes, open-source models will proliferate and capability will increase. But oral defense has no false positive rate *regardless* of LLM capability. A real-time oral examination is not made less effective by GPT-5. Process documentation is not defeated by better paraphrasing. The assessment approaches I advocate are robust to the capability trajectory you describe precisely because they do not compete with AI at the product level — they test the person, not the artifact. The technological landscape you describe is an argument *for* human-centered assessment, not an argument for resignation.

**CT challenges all:** The exchange between ED and TE is the most important one so far, because it converges on the key operational point: assessment redesign and oral defense are the interventions that are (a) empirically supported, (b) not undermined by AI capability improvement, and (c) actionable without waiting for market solutions (RW) or structural transformation (LW). The disagreement is not about the efficacy of these tools — everyone at this table accepts them. The disagreement is about what to do while institutions are redesigning assessments, which takes years, and fraud is happening now.

## ROUND 2 — Detection Tools

**Question:** "Should AI detection tools like Turnitin and GPTZero be used in academic discipline? Given Liang et al.'s 61.3% false positive rate on ESL writers, are they defensible?"

---

**TE:** No, as currently deployed for enforcement. I want to be precise about the technical argument. Turnitin and GPTZero operate on perplexity and burstiness — they measure how statistically predictable a text is by comparison to LLM output distributions. ESL writers systematically produce text with higher predictability (simpler sentence structures, lower lexical diversity, higher-frequency word choices) because that is what learning a second language produces. The detector is not detecting AI — it is detecting non-native English proficiency and calling it AI authorship. Liang et al.'s 61.3% figure versus 1.5% for native speakers is a 40-fold disparity. That is not measurement error — it is a structural property of perplexity-based classification. The tool cannot be recalibrated to eliminate this disparity without abandoning the underlying detection mechanism. As a standalone enforcement instrument against ESL populations, it is technically indefensible.

**LW:** TE has given you the technical case. Let me give you the political economy case. Turnitin's detection module was launched in April 2023. Turnitin's own documentation warned against "sole reliance." Vanderbilt, Northwestern, Michigan State, and UT Austin disabled it after the Liang et al. study. And Turnitin kept selling it. This is the EdTech industry pattern Selwyn documented: tools are deployed commercially before they are validated pedagogically, the vendors profit from the institutional panic they help maintain, and the costs fall on the students with the least power to contest the results — international students, ESL writers, first-generation students who lack the social capital to navigate appeals. The UK Office of the Independent Adjudicator documented a wave of wrongful accusations from 2023 to 2025. This is not a theoretical harm. It is documented institutional discrimination, sold at institutional subscription rates.

**RW:** I agree with both TE and LW that AI detector output cannot serve as primary evidence in academic proceedings. The 61.3% false positive rate for ESL writers is disqualifying for enforcement use. This is one of the places where the left-wing critique of EdTech industry practices is correct: vendors marketing claimed 98% accuracy when independent testing finds 50% real-world performance, specifically against ESL populations, is a product liability and commercial ethics problem. Where I diverge from LW: the answer is not to abandon enforcement — it is to build better institutional procedures that use detection as a soft signal triggering human judgment, not as automated verdict. The Liang et al. finding is an argument for due process reform, not for accepting that all enforcement is discriminatory.

**CT:** The center position is precise here: detection tools may be used as one advisory layer in a multi-signal investigation. No grade penalty may be applied based on detector output alone. Every detection flag must trigger a conversation with the student before any formal proceeding. This is not a radical position — it mirrors best-practice evidentiary jurisprudence, and it is what Turnitin's own guidelines recommend, even as they continue to market the product for enforcement contexts. The CT concern is that the binary framing — deploy detection versus abandon detection — is missing the operational middle ground. The University of Sheffield's 92 confirmed AI cases in 2023-24 were detected through a mix of faculty judgment, tool flags, and stylistic inconsistency identification. Zero-tool detection is not how real institutions operate.

**EC:** From an economic standpoint, the question is expected value. The 61.3% ESL false positive rate means that for every confirmed fraud case detected in a Vietnamese English-medium course, approximately 0.6+ innocent students are wrongfully accused. The false positive costs — faculty time for adjudication, student psychological harm, potential grade appeal proceedings — likely exceed the benefit of true positive detections at this accuracy level. This is a standard decision theory result: when base rates are moderate and false positive costs are high, a classifier with 60%+ false positive rates should not be used as a primary enforcement mechanism. It may have value as a soft signal triggering low-cost human review, but the threshold for that use must be set such that the human review step is not itself a punitive experience.

**ED:** I want to emphasize what TE, LW, EC, and RW have all now agreed on, because it is remarkable: the 61.3% false positive rate is disqualifying for enforcement use. That consensus spans the entire ideological range of this table. What I add from the learning science side is that detection-first approaches don't merely fail technically — they damage the institutional conditions that produce integrity. McCabe's research shows that surveillance cultures produce students skilled at evasion, not students who have internalized integrity as a personal value. Deploying detection tools as the primary institutional response is spending resources on the wrong problem while actively damaging the cultural conditions that would address the right problem.

### Rebuttals — Round 2

**LW challenges CT:** You say "use detection as one advisory layer, not sole evidence." That sounds reasonable. But look at how it operates in practice: a detection flag goes to the instructor, who may have no technical training, no understanding of Liang et al., no knowledge that their Vietnamese student's ESL writing pattern is statistically indistinguishable from GPT-4 output. The "human judgment" layer you rely on is not neutral — it is saturated with the same biases, assumptions, and power asymmetries that the algorithmic layer encodes. The Texas A&M incident was not a case of an algorithm acting autonomously — it was a case of a human (Instructor Mumm) using AI output (ChatGPT evaluating student work) as if it were neutral evidence. Your "human in the loop" is only as good as the training and the incentives of that human.

**CT challenges LW:** Your Texas A&M case is real, but look at what it actually proves. Mumm did not use Turnitin or GPTZero — he used ChatGPT itself, with no institutional protocol and no training. The failure was the absence of institutional procedure, not the existence of detection tools. The University of Sheffield's 92 confirmed cases and the University of Minnesota's 188 cases were handled through proper multi-signal procedures. The answer to Mumm's failure is institutional competence requirements, not abandonment of all detection-assisted review. Your framework predicts that any institution deploying detection will reproduce Texas A&M. The evidence shows that institutions with proper procedures do not.

**TE challenges EC:** Your expected value argument is analytically correct, but I want to sharpen the asymmetry point. In standard statistical decision theory, the acceptable false positive rate depends on the relative costs of Type I and Type II errors. In academic misconduct proceedings, Type I error (falsely accusing an innocent student) has severe, often irreversible consequences — psychological harm, record contamination, chilling effect on honest students from the same demographic. Type II error (missing a case of genuine fraud) is serious but recoverable — the fraudulent credential exists, but can be addressed through downstream competency testing, and the overall detection rate does not need to be 100% for deterrence to operate. Given this asymmetry, the appropriate false positive tolerance is extremely low, perhaps 1-2%. The 61.3% rate is not merely "too high" — it is wrong by a factor of 30.

**RW challenges LW:** You argue that the detection tool market is a corporate ethics failure — Turnitin profiting from the panic it helps generate. This analysis is correct about the incentive structure. But your prescription — skepticism toward market actors, preference for educator-mediated institutional control — does not follow. Turnitin is an incumbent. The right response is not to abandon detection markets but to require independent auditing of tool performance claims, create liability frameworks for misleading accuracy marketing, and allow competition from better tools. The Craver/Emory case — a student suspended for building a legitimate AI study tool — is the paradigmatic failure I oppose: incumbent vendors protected, innovative challengers punished.

**Residual disagreement — operational:** CT and RW argue for "detection as soft advisory signal with human review." The implication — higher risk for Vietnamese students — is credible but not directly measured.

**One genuinely irreconcilable value difference:** LW's position that detection tools constitute systematic institutional discrimination against marginalized students, regardless of procedure, cannot be reconciled with RW/CT's position that well-designed procedures can deploy detection tools fairly. This is a values disagreement about whether institutional procedures can correct for systemic discrimination, not an empirical dispute.

## ROUND 3 — Solutions Clash

**Question:** "What is the single most effective intervention? Assessment redesign, market mechanisms, regulation, or technical solutions?"

---

**ED:** Assessment redesign — without contest. The spot viva combined with process-based staged submissions is the only intervention that is (a) empirically validated (University of Western Ontario 600-student implementation, Macquarie Law School viva voce, University of Sydney Learning Futures), (b) immune to the detection arms race, (c) has no false positive rate when conducted competently, and (d) produces genuine pedagogical benefit independent of its fraud-deterrence effect. The literature on formative assessment — Dylan Wiliam's research base — shows that feedback during the learning process produces substantially larger learning gains than summative evaluation alone. A spot viva is not merely fraud detection. It is the best possible formative learning event: direct engagement with a student's understanding in real time. This intervention solves the fraud problem *and* improves education. Nothing else in this debate can claim both.

**TE:** I agree with ED that assessment redesign is the highest-priority intervention, and I will add the technical reason: it is the only approach that degrades gracefully as LLM capability improves. Every detection approach I can describe — perplexity analysis, behavioral biometrics, process fingerprinting — has a ceiling that future LLMs will breach. Oral defense does not. You cannot deploy GPT-5 to answer follow-up questions on behalf of a student in a room with an examiner. The intervention that remains effective against GPT-5, GPT-6, and open-source Llama successors running locally on student hardware is the intervention that tests the person rather than the artifact.

**EC:** Assessment redesign is the highest-efficiency intervention, and I want to quantify why. The spot viva model at 10% random sampling for a 300-student cohort requires approximately 10 hours of examiner time per assessment cycle. Against this, consider the recurring costs of detection tool licensing (Turnitin institutional subscription), false positive adjudication (faculty time, student psychological costs, potential appeals), and the human capital destruction of undeterred fraud (graduates without genuine competencies entering the labor market). The NPV of oral defense investment is substantially positive. Market mechanisms (RW's credential unbundling) are the correct *long-run* structural fix, but they require 10-15 years of labor market evolution and cannot address the immediate crisis. Regulation (CT's MOET Circular) is the correct *medium-term* mechanism to mandate institutional redesign at scale.

**RW:** Assessment redesign is a sound tactic and I endorse it as a competitive institutional differentiator. But "single most effective intervention" depends on the time horizon. In the five-to-ten-year frame, the deepest structural fix is credential unbundling: when employers can verify competency through portfolios, work samples, and professional examinations independent of university assessment, the return on credential fraud collapses. The University of Western Ontario's oral exam program is excellent. It only affects students who enroll at Western Ontario. The market-structural reform that breaks the employer over-reliance on degree signals changes the incentive calculation for every student at every institution in the economy simultaneously. That is a fundamentally larger leverage point, even if it takes longer.

**LW:** I agree with ED and TE on assessment redesign. But I want to resist the framing of this as a "single most effective intervention" because it lets institutions off the hook. Assessment redesign is necessary and insufficient. The institutions doing the most sophisticated detection deployment — the Sheffield 92 cases, the Minnesota 188 cases — are the ones that have invested least in assessment redesign, not most. The political economy of institutional responses consistently favors the cheap surveillance fix over the expensive pedagogical investment. If "single most effective" means "most likely to actually be implemented," the answer is probably mandatory regulation — CT's MOET Circular — because market competition (RW) and voluntary good practice (ED) both fail to reach the institutions that need change most urgently.

**CT:** The evidence base points to a layered response, not a single intervention. For the "single most effective" question, I will argue for assessment redesign combined with the AIAS framework, specifically because BUV's Hanoi pilot gives us direct Vietnamese evidence that it works. But I want to push back on LW's characterization of regulation as the most reliably implemented intervention. QĐ 1705 was issued in October 2024 and promotes AI adoption in Vietnamese education without a single integrity safeguard. Regulation can be wrong in content and slow in impact. The MOET Circular I proposed is the correct regulatory vehicle, but calling regulation generically the most effective lever ignores the evidence that the relevant regulations in Vietnam are currently pointing in the wrong direction.

### Rebuttals — Round 3

**LW challenges RW:** You say credential unbundling is the "deepest structural fix." Google, Apple, and IBM have been running Skills First hiring programs for several years now. The credential premium in Vietnam remains high and rising. The empirical evidence for credential unbundling actually changing the incentive calculation for developing-country students, in labor markets where the university degree remains the primary sorting mechanism, is essentially zero. You are advocating for a solution that works in California for software engineers and presenting it as a universal answer for Vietnamese agriculture graduates or public school teachers. The specificity matters.

**RW challenges LW:** You say "mandatory regulation is most likely to be implemented" while simultaneously pointing out that QĐ 1705 was issued without integrity safeguards. That is the problem with regulation-as-solution: it is only as good as its content, and the regulators making the relevant decisions in Vietnam are the same MOET officials who issued an AI adoption strategy with no integrity provisions. You cannot consistently argue that market actors are unreliable and regulatory actors are reliable — both are subject to capture, incentive misalignment, and competence failures. The BUV AIAS pilot worked because Perkins is a skilled educator who implemented it well, not because it was mandated from above.

**EC challenges RW:** On credential unbundling — the economic argument is internally sound but the transition path is the problem. In Spence's signaling model, employers adopt new signals only when the information they convey is reliable and legible. Portfolio credentials and work-sample tests require employer investment in new evaluation infrastructure. In a developing economy context with high informal sector employment and limited HR capacity in most firms, this infrastructure does not materialize automatically from market incentives. The empirical evidence on employer adoption of alternative credentials outside high-income tech sectors is thin. Your solution works in the sector where it is least needed.

**TE challenges CT:** You advocate for assessment redesign plus AIAS as the single most effective intervention. I agree with the substance. But I want to flag the open-source model problem that none of the regulatory or pedagogical solutions addresses. By 2028, students will have access to locally-run, highly capable, completely untrackable LLMs — Llama and Mistral successors running on personal hardware, unaffected by any institutional policy, any watermarking mandate, any detection tool. The assessment approaches that will remain effective are specifically those that do not rely on detecting AI output — oral defense, real-time supervised production, process documentation. Any intervention that relies on detecting or blocking AI access will fail against this trajectory. This is not a critique of AIAS — AIAS is agnostic to detection. It is a critique of any framework that assigns significant weight to detection as a component.

## ROUND 4 — Vietnam and the Developing World

**Question:** "Given Vietnam's specific context — Decree 109/2022's AI gap, high credential premium, ESL majority students, tự chủ đại học pressures — what should Vietnamese institutions do RIGHT NOW?"

---

**CT:** Three immediate actions, none requiring legislation. First: every Vietnamese HEI with international or English-medium programs should immediately suspend AI detection tool use as a *sole evidence* basis for academic misconduct findings. This is implementable this week. It requires no new policy — it requires applying the tools as their own vendors recommend. Second: adopt the AIAS framework for all new course syllabus design. BUV has already piloted this in Hanoi; the framework is translated into Vietnamese; the operational model exists. Third: issue MOET's ministerial circular bridging Decree 109's integrity categories and QĐ 1705's AI adoption mandate. This is the minimum viable regulatory action. It does not require amending Decree 109 — a circular is a faster instrument.

**EC:** The economic analysis maps directly to Vietnam's specific vulnerabilities. The credential premium is high, meaning the benefit-of-fraud term in the cost-benefit calculation is larger than in high-income countries. The ESL false positive problem makes detection tools particularly dangerous here — Vietnamese students writing in English face the documented 61.3% false positive risk without the institutional advocacy infrastructure that TOEFL-exam populations have access to. The tự chủ đại học (university autonomy) reforms introduced performance management frameworks that can reproduce publish-or-perish pressures domestically. Immediate actions: mandate 30% of summative assessment through AI-resistant methods (oral, real-time production, process documentation) via MOET Circular by September 2026; standardize penalty schedules nationally so the HCMUFA 50% deduction anomaly — softest in the Asian peer set — cannot be exploited as a lenient-institution arbitrage.

**ED:** From a pedagogical standpoint, what Vietnamese educators need most urgently is not policy — it is professional development. Faculty are being asked to navigate a transformed assessment landscape without training, time, or institutional support. The assessment redesign prescriptions CT and EC describe are correct, but they require educators who know how to implement spot vivas, how to design staged process portfolios, how to use Google Docs revision history as assessment evidence. None of this is difficult, but none of it happens without structured professional development. MOET's QĐ 1705 already mandates teacher capacity development — this is the policy hook to fund and require AI-era assessment design training. Start there. An unfunded circular is policy theater.

**RW:** I will flag a specific Vietnam-relevant structural problem that others have underemphasized: tự chủ đại học pressures. University autonomy expansion in Vietnam has introduced performance management frameworks — publication targets, enrollment growth incentives, international ranking aspirations — that create exactly the publish-or-perish incentive structures at the domestic level that drove the Hindawi retraction catastrophe internationally. Vietnamese universities operating under autonomy models are potentially replicating the same incentive distortions. Immediate action from a market-oriented perspective: require Vietnamese HEIs to publish graduate employment outcome data at the program level, disaggregated by actual competency indicators. This creates market accountability pressure for genuine learning outcomes without waiting for regulatory mandates that are slow and potentially captured.

**LW:** I want to put on the table the equity issue that is most concrete for Vietnam right now. Vietnamese students on English-medium programs are an ESL population facing the documented 40-fold false positive disparity if detection tools are deployed against their writing. This is not hypothetical. MOET's 2025 internationalization push under QĐ 1705 is expanding English-medium instruction precisely as EdTech vendors are marketing AI detection tools to Vietnamese institutions. The combination is predictable: Vietnamese institutions adopt detection tools to demonstrate compliance with international academic standards, Vietnamese ESL students are disproportionately flagged, and the institutions that least need additional injustice burden receive it most. Immediate action: MOET should explicitly prohibit use of AI detection tools trained on native-English corpora as evidence in Vietnamese academic misconduct proceedings until bias auditing for Vietnamese-English-medium student writing has been conducted. This is a specific, concrete, justifiable prohibition that can be issued immediately.

**TE:** I will add the technical forward-looking point for Vietnamese institutions: any integrity infrastructure you build now should be designed for the 2028 threat landscape, not the 2023 one. The detection-based approaches some Vietnamese institutions are currently considering — purchasing Turnitin subscriptions, building detection workflows — will be operationally obsolete within three years as open-source capable models proliferate on personal devices. The assessment infrastructure that remains robust: oral defense, real-time supervised production, process documentation. These should be the priority for institutional investment. The Vietnamese institutions that build oral defense and AIAS-based assessment infrastructure now will be ahead of the curve. Those that invest in detection infrastructure will be paying for something that stops working.

### Rebuttals — Round 4

Students cheating now will have graduated before any labor market feedback reaches institutional behavior. ### Moderator's Assessment — Round 4

This is consensus across the full ideological range.

Both are empirically defensible; the disagreement reflects different assumptions about how institutional change occurs in Vietnamese HE contexts.
