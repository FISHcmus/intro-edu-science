# AI Fraud in Education: A Professional Educator's Position Paper

**Author perspective:** Professional educator and education researcher  
**Discipline background:** Learning science, curriculum design, pedagogical theory  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Context:** Prepared for EdTech research seminar — academic integrity in the AI era

---

## Opening Thesis

The crisis of AI-assisted academic fraud that has convulsed higher education since 2022 is real, documented, and serious. But it is not the crisis we think it is. The spread of ChatGPT-generated essays, hallucinated citations, and AI-contaminated research papers does not primarily reveal a generation of dishonest students or a collapse of academic values. It reveals something educators should have confronted a decade ago: that our assessment systems were already broken before AI arrived. Generative AI merely made the dysfunction visible at scale.

This position paper argues from the perspective of learning science and pedagogical theory. My starting premise, grounded in the work of Tricia Bertram Gallant (2008), Donald McCabe (2012), and Sarah Elaine Eaton (2021, 2024), is that academic dishonesty is a systemic symptom of institutional failure — of assessment designs that have never genuinely measured what they claim to measure. My operating principle is Rose Luckin's (UCL) framework for intelligence augmentation: AI can be a powerful ally in developing genuine human understanding, provided we design learning environments that demand authentic intellectual engagement. My skeptical lens is Neil Selwyn's (Monash) insistence that we interrogate who benefits from every technological intervention and at whose expense.

From this vantage point, I will defend specific positions: what I accept, what I reject, what I consider non-negotiable, and what concrete changes I recommend.

## 1. The Pedagogical Root Cause: Broken Assessment, Not Broken Students

The most important insight in academic integrity research is also the most consistently ignored by administrators: students who cheat are, in large measure, responding rationally to assessment environments that do not require genuine learning to succeed.

Gallant's 2008 ASHE monograph, *Academic Integrity in the Twenty-First Century*, established the foundational systemic critique. She documented what anyone who has taught in a large lecture course already knows intuitively: when the assessment is an anonymous multiple-choice exam, a five-paragraph essay on a generic topic, or a problem set with answers available online, the institutional environment structures cheating as the path of least effort with minimal personal cost. The student who cheats on a generic essay prompt about "the causes of World War One" is not failing morally — the assessment itself is failing pedagogically. It was not measuring genuine historical reasoning. It was measuring compliance with a ritualized form.

McCabe's longitudinal survey data (2012), drawn from 70,000 students across more than 100 US institutions, confirmed this structural reading: approximately two-thirds of college students self-reported at least one instance of cheating. This is not a moral collapse. This is a systems signal. When behavior is this widespread, the proper analytical response is not to indict the individuals but to examine the system producing it.

Generative AI has simply raised the stakes. Newton and Xiromeriti (2023) demonstrated empirically that ChatGPT can pass assessments across multiple disciplines — medicine, law, engineering — without triggering conventional detection. This is not surprising. Any assessment that a large language model can pass without genuine understanding was not testing genuine understanding to begin with. The LLM has performed a service: it has revealed, with stark precision, exactly which assessments were measuring retrieval and surface performance rather than deep learning.

Eaton's "postplagiarism" framework (2021, 2023) captures the conceptual shift clearly. When an AI generates text, there is no human "original" to plagiarize from — the ethical violation is not copying but *misrepresentation of authorship*. This reconceptualization matters because it redirects our attention from the artifact (the suspicious text) to the relationship (the student's actual understanding). Did the student engage intellectually? Can they account for the ideas? These are pedagogical questions, not forensic ones.

The practical implication is blunt: any institutional response to AI fraud that does not begin with assessment redesign is not addressing the cause. It is managing the symptoms.

## 2. What Good Assessment Looks Like

Good assessment in the AI era is not fundamentally different from what good assessment has always been. The AI crisis has simply made the principles non-optional. Here is what learning science tells us about assessment that genuinely measures learning:

### 2.1 Process-Based Assessment

Assessment should evaluate *how learning happened*, not only what was produced. Process-based assessment — staged submissions, reflective journals, annotated bibliographies, Google Docs revision histories — requires evidence of authentic intellectual work over time. An AI cannot convincingly fabricate a documented learning trajectory: genuine revisions, dead ends, reconsidered arguments, and metacognitive reflection.

The University of Sydney's Learning Futures program represents the most comprehensive institutional implementation: faculty workshops, standardized staged submission templates, LMS integration, and student orientation materials. Stanford's 2023 policy updates added process documentation requirements to high-stakes assessments. The Chartered College of Teaching UK published practical guidance for subject-area educators in 2023.

Process-based approaches align directly with Vygotsky's foundational insight, developed extensively by Luckin (2018): intelligence is not a static product but a developmental process of social interaction and internalization. Assessment that measures only final products measures only the most impoverished dimension of learning. Assessment that traces the development of understanding over time measures what education is actually supposed to produce.

### 2.2 Oral Defense and Spot Vivas

The oldest assessment method is also the most AI-resistant: ask the student to explain, justify, and defend their work in real time. No LLM can stand in for a student in a room with an examiner asking direct questions about the reasoning behind submitted work.

The "spot viva" model — randomly sampling 10–20% of written submissions for a 15–20 minute oral follow-up — is the most cost-effective scalable implementation. The University of Western Ontario applied it to a cohort of 600 undergraduate business students in 2023–2024. Macquarie University Law School formalized it for undergraduate law assessment, reporting improved student engagement and employability outcomes alongside integrity benefits. University of Sydney and UC San Diego have incorporated oral components into their post-2022 assessment redesign frameworks.

The spot viva has a property that no detection tool possesses: it has no false positive rate when conducted competently. A student who engaged genuinely with their work can explain it. A student who did not cannot. The detector is human judgment applied to authentic intellectual performance, not algorithmic pattern-matching applied to text artifacts.

This method also embodies Luckin's distinction between "AI as tutor" and "AI as substitute." A student who used AI to explore ideas, test arguments, and deepen understanding during the writing process can demonstrate that understanding orally. A student who used AI to produce the work without engagement cannot. The oral defense resolves the distinction at the point of assessment — it does not require us to reconstruct how the student used AI during composition.

### 2.3 The AIAS 5-Tier Framework

Mike Perkins (British University Vietnam) developed the AI Assessment Scale (AIAS) as a practical institutional tool: a five-level framework ranging from "no AI permitted" (Level 1) to "full AI integration with human oversight and attribution" (Level 5). The framework's core innovation is clarity: instead of a binary prohibition that produces ambiguity and underground use, it gives educators a vocabulary for calibrating AI permissions to learning objectives.

The AIAS has been adopted by hundreds of institutions globally, translated into more than 30 languages, and recognized by TEQSA (Australia) in 2024. BUV's pilot demonstrated measurable reduction in AI-related misconduct cases and improved student engagement. The framework operationalizes Eaton's postplagiarism principle: what matters is not whether AI was used but whether its use was transparent, attributed, and bounded by clear disclosure norms.

For educators designing assessments, the AIAS provides a decision framework: what does this assignment require the student to demonstrate? At which AIAS level does that learning objective make sense? Level 1 (no AI) is appropriate for assessments requiring personally situated reflection. Level 3 or 4 may be appropriate for collaborative research tasks where AI-assisted literature synthesis is disclosed and verified. The framework does not eliminate faculty judgment — it structures it.

### 2.4 Portfolio and Authentic Assessment

Portfolio assessment — a curated collection of work demonstrating development over time, with student reflection on that development — is among the most reliable measures of genuine competency growth. It is also structurally resistant to AI substitution: the coherence of a portfolio depends on demonstrated intellectual development across multiple time points, connected by a student's own metacognitive narrative.

Authentic assessment — tasks embedded in real-world contexts with genuine stakes — makes the question "why would I cheat on this?" psychologically more difficult. Project-based learning, community-engaged inquiry, case studies drawn from actual professional contexts, and capstone work connected to students' own interests all reduce the felt distance between "doing the assignment" and "actually learning something."

## 3. McCabe's Honor Culture Research: Why Culture Outperforms Enforcement

Donald McCabe's most important finding is also his most counterintuitive to administrators who believe enforcement is the answer: institutions with strong honor code cultures show 25–30% lower self-reported cheating rates than comparable institutions without such cultures. This is not a marginal effect. It is the largest effect size in the academic integrity literature.

McCabe distinguished between "modified honor codes" (students pledge; little else changes) and "full honor codes" (students pledge; faculty treat students as trusted partners; institutional norms consistently model integrity; even examinations may be unproctored because the culture makes cheating socially costly). It is the full honor code model — the cultural implementation, not the ceremonial pledge — that produces the integrity dividend.

The mechanism is not fear of punishment. McCabe's data showed that students at honor code institutions *think differently* about academic integrity — they have internalized an understanding of why it matters, not just what the rules say. They experience academic work as personally meaningful rather than as a performance for evaluation. Cheating becomes not just risky but conceptually at odds with how they understand themselves as learners.

This finding translates directly to the AI era with one modification that Eaton (2024) makes explicit: the honor code culture must explicitly address AI use as a dimension of academic integrity, not treat it as a separate technical compliance issue. An institution that builds a culture of intellectual honesty — where AI use is disclosed, attributed, and evaluated pedagogically — is more likely to produce students who engage with AI ethically than an institution that deploys surveillance technology while leaving the underlying culture unreformed.

McCabe's data is also a rebuke to detection-first approaches. He was consistently skeptical that enforcement alone produced behavioral change. Detection tools (Turnitin, GPTZero) may catch violations, but they do not reduce the motivation to violate. Culture reduces motivation. Detection responds to violation. These are different problems requiring different solutions, and the academic integrity literature is clear about which one has larger leverage.

The practical implication for institutions: invest in the culture-building infrastructure McCabe identified — faculty modeling of integrity values, student orientation that explains *why* integrity matters rather than just what is prohibited, visible institutional commitment from leadership, and a community rather than compliance framing. Then add assessment redesign. Then, if warranted, add detection tools as a last layer — not the first.

## 4. Teacher Professional Development: What Educators Need Now

The most underinvested dimension of the AI academic integrity crisis is educator preparation. Faculty are being asked to navigate a transformed assessment landscape without the training, time, or institutional support to do so effectively. This is professionally unjust and pedagogically counterproductive.

What teachers need in the AI era falls into four categories:

**Assessment redesign competency.** Most educators were trained to design assessments in a pre-AI world. They need specific, practical guidance on: what makes an assessment AI-resistant (or appropriately AI-permissive); how to implement process-based and portfolio approaches in their discipline; how to design spot viva questions and rubrics; and how to calibrate AIAS levels to learning objectives. This is not a one-hour workshop. It is sustained professional development with follow-up, peer support, and institutional time allocation.

**AI literacy — technical and critical.** Educators need to understand what large language models can and cannot do: how they generate text, where they hallucinate, what patterns are characteristic of AI-generated writing, and how detection tools work and where they fail. This is the technical dimension. The critical dimension is equally important: Selwyn's (Distrusting Educational Technology, 2014) framework for asking "who benefits, who is harmed, and what structural interests are served" by every new EdTech tool is not pessimism — it is professional responsibility. Teachers who can evaluate EdTech claims critically are better positioned to protect their students.

**Eaton's postplagiarism framework as conceptual vocabulary.** Educators need a shared conceptual framework for thinking about human-AI hybrid authorship. The question "did the student plagiarize?" has a clear answer when the source is another human's text. The question "did the student misrepresent their authorship in a postplagiarism context?" is more complex and requires new thinking. Eaton's framework provides that vocabulary.

**Trust-based relationship skills.** Luckin (2018) argues that the Zone of Proximal Development — the pedagogically fertile space where genuine learning happens — requires a trust relationship between learner and educator. Detection-first cultures damage that relationship. Educators need to be supported in building the trust-based learning relationships that make authentic assessment possible, rather than being asked to function as forensic investigators.

## 5. What I Accept: Technology That Genuinely Supports Learning

I am not opposed to educational technology. I am opposed to technology deployed without pedagogical justification, and I am opposed to surveillance masquerading as support. Here is what I accept:

**Khan Academy's Khanmigo model.** Khanmigo is designed as a Socratic tutor: it asks questions rather than providing answers; it guides students to work through problems rather than completing work for them; it is explicitly prohibited from writing essays on a student's behalf. This is AI designed from pedagogical principles — it extends the Zone of Proximal Development rather than bypassing it. The Khanmigo model makes the learning process visible and interactive. I accept this model as the appropriate template for AI in education: AI as a more patient, more available version of a skilled tutor's probing questions.

**Formative AI feedback.** AI tools that provide immediate, specific, and actionable feedback on drafts — identifying structural weaknesses, questioning unsupported claims, suggesting areas for further research — function as formative assessment support. Dylan Wiliam's extensive research on formative assessment demonstrates that feedback during the learning process produces substantially larger learning gains than summative evaluation alone. AI that supports this feedback loop without substituting for student thinking is genuinely valuable.

**Process-documentation tools.** Google Docs revision history, Moodle assignment logging, and LMS analytics that make the learning process visible are useful infrastructure for process-based assessment. These tools are not surveillance; they are documentation of authentic learning. Students who use Google Docs honestly produce revision histories that demonstrate genuine intellectual work. The documentation supports the pedagogical relationship rather than replacing faculty judgment.

**AI literacy as curriculum content.** Teaching students how AI systems work, what their limitations are, how to use them ethically, and how to evaluate AI-generated content critically is legitimate curriculum content in every discipline. This is not a concession to AI fraud — it is preparation for a world in which professional life will involve AI tools. The question is whether students learn to use these tools with critical intelligence or in unreflective dependence.

## 6. What I Reject: Detection-First Approaches and Punitive Cultures

I reject detection-first responses to AI fraud as both ineffective and harmful.

**The false positive problem is not a technical glitch — it is a structural injustice.** Liang et al. (Stanford, 2023) documented that AI detection tools show significantly elevated false positive rates for non-native English writers. A study of AI detection on texts by proficient ESL writers found that their natural writing patterns — more formal vocabulary, more careful sentence construction, avoidance of idiomatic expressions — are systematically misclassified as AI-generated. In the Vietnamese higher education context, where English-language academic writing is common but most students are non-native speakers, deploying Turnitin AI Detection or GPTZero as a primary integrity mechanism would disproportionately accuse the most conscientious students of cheating. This is not an acceptable risk.

The Texas A&M case (May 2023) illustrates the institutional danger: an instructor failed an entire class based on AI detection software output, without individual review, without due process, without appeal. Many students in that class had not used AI. The software was wrong. The institutional damage — to trust, to the affected students' records, to the credibility of AI detection as a policy tool — was real and serious. This is what happens when detection tools are treated as definitive rather than as one imperfect signal among many.

**Surveillance damages the trust that makes learning possible.** Selwyn's critical sociology of education technology provides the analytical framework: when institutions deploy surveillance tools, they are making a statement about the teacher-student relationship. They are saying: we do not trust you; we treat you as a suspect until proven innocent; the relationship here is adversarial rather than collaborative. This is not an abstract concern. McCabe's research shows that institutional culture — including the implicit messages conveyed by how integrity is enforced — shapes student behavior. A culture of surveillance produces students who are skilled at evasion, not students who have internalized integrity as a personal value.

Luckin (2018) makes the same point from a learning science perspective: Vygotskian social learning theory requires that the relationship between learner and educator be one of productive challenge and support. Detection-first cultures replace this with an adversarial dynamic that is antithetical to the conditions under which genuine learning happens.

**Punitive responses to symptoms do not address causes.** Institutions that respond to AI fraud primarily by establishing elaborate disciplinary procedures — AI misconduct committees, escalating penalty tiers, expulsion for serious cases — are spending institutional energy on consequences rather than on the assessment redesign that would reduce fraud in the first place. CUHK's 2023 policy of potential expulsion for AI fraud represents a defensible position given its institutional context, but expulsion does not redesign the assessments that made fraud attractive.

**The arms race is unwinnable.** Academic integrity researchers, including Newton (2023) and Lancaster (Imperial College), have consistently documented that detection technology lags behind evasion technology. The timeline runs as follows: AI generates detectable text → detection tools are trained to identify it → prompt engineering and "humanization" tools are developed to evade detection → improved detection is developed → and so on. Each cycle requires institutional investment and each cycle produces false positives affecting innocent students. The endpoint of the arms race is not a detection tool that works reliably — it is an ever-escalating adversarial dynamic with no pedagogical benefit.

The alternative to winning the arms race is to exit it. Process-based assessment, oral defense, and authentic tasks do not require detection because they require the student's genuine intellectual presence in ways that cannot be substituted.

## 7. My Red Lines: Pedagogical Non-Negotiables

These are the principles I hold as non-negotiable in any institutional response to AI fraud:

**Non-negotiable 1: Assessment must measure learning, not compliance.** Any assessment response to AI fraud that leaves intact assessments which can be completed by AI without genuine learning is not an academic integrity response — it is security theater. If an LLM can pass an assessment without understanding, the assessment was not measuring understanding. That is the primary problem to be solved.

**Non-negotiable 2: Due process is not optional.** No student may be accused of AI misconduct, penalized, or have their record affected based solely on algorithmic detection output. Every AI detection flag requires human review, consideration of individual context, opportunity for the student to respond, and a proportionate range of responses. The Texas A&M precedent must not be replicated.

**Non-negotiable 3: ESL students must not bear a disproportionate integrity burden.** Institutional policies must account for the documented false positive bias of AI detection tools against non-native English writers. In multilingual educational contexts — which describes most of the world's higher education — this is not a marginal concern. It is a central equity issue.

**Non-negotiable 4: The teacher-student trust relationship must be protected.** Surveillance technologies deployed without student consent, without transparency about how they work and what data they collect, and without clear limits on their use in disciplinary proceedings are an unacceptable intrusion into the pedagogical relationship. Selwyn's critical framework applies here: we must ask who benefits from this surveillance and who bears its costs.

**Non-negotiable 5: AI literacy must be a curriculum right, not an afterthought.** Students have a right to understand the tools they are being asked to use ethically. Institutions cannot legitimately prohibit, permit, or regulate AI use without also ensuring that students have the conceptual and technical literacy to comply with those regulations intelligently.

**Non-negotiable 6: Teachers must be resourced, not blamed.** The faculty member who lacks the time, training, or institutional support to redesign assessments is not failing professionally — the institution is failing that faculty member. Professional development in AI-era assessment design must be funded, timetabled, and recognized.

## 8. Specific Curriculum Proposals: Concrete Assessment Redesign Recommendations

These recommendations are grounded in the evidence reviewed above. They are ordered from most urgent to most structural.

### Recommendation 1: Implement Spot Viva for High-Stakes Written Assessments

For any written assessment worth more than 20% of a course grade, implement random spot viva sampling at 10–20%. Each selected student attends a 15–20 minute oral follow-up within two weeks of submission. Examiners ask questions drawn directly from the submitted work: explain your reasoning for this argument; what would complicate the claim you made in section three; where did you encounter the most difficulty in this analysis?

The institutional investment is approximately 10 hours of examiner time per 300-student course per assessment cycle at 10% sampling — manageable with appropriate TA training and scheduling infrastructure. The behavioral effect operates at the assignment design level: students who know a spot viva is possible are incentivized to engage genuinely with material, not just to produce a credible artifact.

Design principle: frame the spot viva to students as a learning opportunity, not an interrogation. "We want to discuss your work with you" produces a different relational dynamic than "we are checking whether you cheated." McCabe's culture research supports this framing choice.

### Recommendation 2: Replace Single-Submission Essays with Staged Process Portfolios

For major written assignments, restructure as follows: topic proposal (week 2) → annotated bibliography with reflection on source relevance (week 4) → outline with argument map (week 6) → first draft with written reflection on what changed from outline to draft (week 8) → peer-reviewed revised draft (week 10) → final submission with a 500-word metacognitive reflection (week 12).

Each stage is graded at low weight (5–10%); the final submission is graded at higher weight. The metacognitive reflections should use prompts that are personally situated: "What evidence changed your initial argument? What claim did you have to abandon and why? What would you do differently?" These prompts are structurally resistant to AI generalization — they require personal, timestamped intellectual history.

Google Docs shared documents provide revision history at no additional cost for institutions using Google Workspace for Education. Moodle's assignment submission logging provides process documentation in LMS environments.

### Recommendation 3: Adopt the AIAS Framework Institutionally

Every course syllabus should specify the AIAS level for each assessment. Level 1 ("No AI") is appropriate for reflective, personally situated tasks and examinations. Level 2 ("AI use for brainstorming only") is appropriate for initial research phases. Level 3 ("AI for drafting assistance with disclosure") is appropriate for collaborative or research-intensive assignments. Levels 4–5 are appropriate for professional practice simulations where AI tool use is a learning objective.

The benefit of AIAS is clarity: students know exactly what is permitted and the boundary between legitimate use and fraud is unambiguous. Perkins' BUV pilot demonstrated that ambiguity about AI rules is a primary driver of AI misuse — students use AI dishonestly not because they have no values but because they are operating in a policy vacuum.

Pair AIAS level declaration with a mandatory AI use disclosure form as part of final submission: "I declare that [no AI / AI for X purpose only / AI for Y and Z purposes as specified] was used in preparing this work." False declaration is explicitly identified as a serious integrity violation. Disclosure, not prohibition, is the principle.

### Recommendation 4: Build AI Literacy as a Standalone Module in Foundational Courses

Every first-year undergraduate student should complete a two-week AI literacy module covering: how large language models work and what they cannot do; documented cases of AI hallucination and citation fabrication (NeurIPS 2025, Hindawi retractions); disclosure and attribution norms; and practical exercises using AI tools in ways that are pedagogically defensible. The module should culminate in a reflective essay: "How did using AI in this exercise change — or not change — my understanding of the subject matter?"

This module is not about compliance with AI rules. It is about developing the critical intelligence to use AI tools as Luckin envisions — as augmentation of human understanding rather than as substitutes for it.

### Recommendation 5: Redesign Examinations Toward Application and Contextualization

If examinations must be retained as summative assessment, they should test application, not recall. "What does this author argue?" can be answered by AI. "This case exhibits these specific features — what does this theoretical framework predict, and what does the discrepancy between the prediction and the actual outcome reveal about the framework's limitations?" requires genuine understanding that must be applied to a novel problem in real time.

Open-book, open-note examinations shift the assessment toward higher-order thinking — precisely the cognitive work that AI supports but cannot substitute for. The closed-book memorization exam was already a poor measure of competency in a world where all information is a search query away. The AI era makes this design flaw terminal.

### Recommendation 6: Faculty Professional Development — A Three-Year Commitment

Institutions should invest in a structured three-year faculty development program in AI-era assessment design: Year 1 focused on understanding AI capabilities and limitations, assessment audit (identify which current assessments are AI-vulnerable), and introduction to process-based approaches. Year 2 focused on piloting redesigned assessments in selected courses with structured peer feedback and student data. Year 3 focused on scaling successful models, publishing internal case studies, and developing departmental assessment design guidelines.

This investment is not optional. It is the institutional commitment that turns policy statements into pedagogical practice.

## Conclusion: The Real Opportunity

The AI academic fraud crisis is, from a certain angle, an opportunity. It has forced into visibility what learning scientists and assessment researchers have argued for decades: that much of what we call assessment was not measuring learning. It has created institutional urgency for the pedagogical reforms that were always justified by the evidence but rarely politically possible.

Luckin (2018) closes with a formulation I find compelling: AI can help humans move intelligence precisely because it forces us to clarify what human intelligence is. When we ask "what can AI not do?", we are asking "what is specifically human about understanding?" The answer — contextual judgment, metacognitive reflection, authentic engagement with ambiguity, the social and emotional dimensions of knowing — describes exactly what good education has always been trying to develop, and exactly what good assessment should always have been measuring.

Selwyn's caution is equally necessary: this opportunity will be captured by commercial interests and managed through surveillance if educators do not insist on their professional authority to define what learning is and how it should be assessed. The EdTech market is already populated with AI detection tools, AI proctoring systems, and AI-generated assessment platforms, each promising institutional security at scale. Some of these tools are genuinely useful as one layer in a multi-pronged approach. None of them is a substitute for the hard, time-consuming, relationship-dependent work of teaching and learning.

McCabe's honor code data tells us that institutional culture is the most powerful variable in academic integrity. The culture we should be building is not one of surveillance and detection. It is one where students understand why intellectual honesty matters — because the genuine competencies they develop are the only things they will carry into the world when no one is checking. That culture is built by assessment design that demands authentic engagement, by faculty who model intellectual integrity, by institutions that invest in learning rather than in monitoring.

The AI fraud crisis is a diagnostic. The treatment is better education.
