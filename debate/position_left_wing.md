# The Rigged Exam: Academic Integrity, AI Fraud, and the Politics of EdTech Surveillance

**Position Paper — Left-Wing Progressive / Critical EdTech Perspective**

*Grounded in: Selwyn (2014) *Distrusting Educational Technology*; Selwyn (2019) *Should Robots Replace Teachers?*; Eaton (2021, 2024); Liang et al. (Stanford, 2023); Gallant (2008); McCabe et al. (2012); research report on liêm chính học thuật và gian lận AI trong EdTech.*

---

## Framing the Problem: Who Gets to Ask the Question?

Before diagnosing "academic integrity," we must ask: who owns the question? Integrity discourse in higher education has always been shaped by those who already hold institutional power — administrators, publishers, accreditation bodies, and increasingly, EdTech corporations with billion-dollar interests in selling detection software. Neil Selwyn (2014) opens *Distrusting Educational Technology* with a disarmingly honest confession: the field of educational technology is shot through with ideology, with "boosterist" claims masquerading as neutral technical solutions, and with a systematic failure to ask whose interests are actually being served when digital tools are adopted in education.

That critique lands with full force in 2026. The "AI fraud crisis" is real — tôi do not dispute that. But the dominant institutional response — mass deployment of AI detection tools, automated punishment pipelines, zero-tolerance expulsion policies — reproduces exactly the pattern Selwyn identified: powerful actors deploying technology in ways that benefit themselves while doing harm to those least able to resist. To engage seriously with academic integrity and AI, we have to refuse the false choice between "do nothing" and "surveil everything." A third path exists. It requires structural honesty that most academic institutions are not yet willing to muster.

---

## I. Root Cause Diagnosis: Why Does AI Fraud Happen?

The technocratic framing treats AI fraud as a supply-side problem: students have access to ChatGPT, therefore they cheat. The solution is detection. This analysis is fundamentally wrong — not because AI use is harmless, but because it mistakes symptom for cause.

**1. The credential economy manufactures cheating.**

Tricia Bertram Gallant (2008) demonstrated with systematic evidence what critical educators have known for decades: academic dishonesty is primarily a symptom of institutional failure, not individual moral depravity. When assessments are poorly designed, when assignments are generic and detached from real learning, when grades are the only commodity that matters, students calculate rationally that the cost of not cheating outweighs the cost of cheating. McCabe, Butterfield, and Treviño (2012) surveyed over 70,000 US college students and found two-thirds self-reported at least one instance of dishonesty. Two-thirds. That is not a story about bad apples. That is a story about a broken barrel.

The Springer 2024 study of 1,386 Vietnamese university students (doi:10.1007/s10639-024-12495-4) found that actual AI fraud rates were approximately three times higher than self-reported rates — a finding entirely consistent with Gallant's under-reporting thesis. Vietnamese higher education operates under some of the most extreme credential pressures in the world: the THPT university entrance examination, hyper-competitive graduate labor markets, family investment in degrees as economic lifelines. When education is primarily a mechanism for sorting individuals into labor market positions, and when the sorting mechanism places overwhelming pressure on final-product assessments (essays, exams, dissertations) rather than genuine competency development, AI fraud is the predictable rational response.

**2. The neoliberal university creates moral conditions for fraud.**

The commercialization of higher education — tuition fees, debt, credential inflation, the reduction of education to "human capital investment" — creates what we might call the fraud-ready student: someone who is simultaneously under enormous financial pressure to succeed, studying material that feels disconnected from real life, in a system where the educator-student relationship has been replaced by a consumer-provider transaction. Selwyn (2014) documents how the EdTech industry exploits precisely this condition: it sells technological solutions to problems that the same educational market has manufactured.

When a student in a 300-person lecture hall, taking a course they are required to complete for accreditation purposes rather than genuine interest, submits an AI-generated essay for a generic "analyze this concept" prompt, they are not a villain. They are an actor in a system that has consistently signaled that what matters is the credential, not the learning. The moral responsibility is widely distributed — and the institution bears a larger share than the student.

**3. Meaningless assessment is the most important enabler.**

Gallant's central argument deserves to be placed at the center of every discussion of AI fraud: if an AI can trivially complete your assignment, your assignment was not testing anything worth testing. The viral observation that AI can pass bar exams, medical boards, and MBA dissertations is not primarily an AI story — it is an assessment design story. These assessments were already measuring the wrong things. AI has simply made the failure legible.

Sarah Eaton's "postplagiarism" framework (2021, 2023) adds conceptual depth: when AI can generate text indistinguishable from human work, the traditional plagiarism framework breaks down at its foundations. The ethical violation shifts from *copying someone else's work* to *misrepresenting authorship*. This is a genuine and important distinction — but it also forces us to ask what authorship means in a world of hybrid human-AI writing, and whether the institutional obsession with individual authorship is itself a symptom of a deeper ideological commitment to competitive individualism over collaborative knowledge-building.

## II. The Surveillance Critique: AI Detection Tools Are a Civil Rights Problem

This is where tôi plant a flag and refuse to move.

The deployment of AI detection tools — Turnitin AI Detection, GPTZero, Copyleaks, and their kin — as instruments of academic discipline is not merely technically flawed. It is a **systemic harm** that disproportionately damages the most vulnerable students, and it is being sold by corporations profiting from exactly the panic they help generate.

**The Liang et al. finding is not a minor caveat. It is disqualifying.**

In April 2023, Weixin Liang, Mert Yuksekgonul, Yining Mao, Eric Wu, and James Zou at Stanford University published a peer-reviewed study in *Patterns* (Cell Press, doi:10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779) that deserves to be read and re-read by every administrator who has deployed these tools. Testing seven of the most widely used AI detectors against authentic TOEFL essays written by Chinese college students, they found a **61.3% false positive rate** — meaning nearly two in three legitimate essays by non-native English speakers were flagged as AI-generated. For native English speakers, the false positive rate was 1.5%.

Let that ratio sink in. A detection system that flags non-native speakers at 40 times the rate of native speakers is not a detection system. It is a discrimination machine. The technical reason is obvious once stated: AI language models and ESL writers share a statistical profile — simpler vocabulary, lower lexical diversity, higher-frequency words, reduced syntactic complexity. Detectors trained to find AI text find ESL text too. This is not a bug to be patched. It is a structural property of the current generation of perplexity-based detection.

The consequences are not abstract. The Texas A&M case of May 2023 — in which an instructor failed an entire class based on AI detection results that were later found to be erroneous — illustrates what happens when these tools are deployed without critical scrutiny. The UK Office of the Independent Adjudicator received a wave of complaints from 2023 to 2025 about students wrongly accused, their academic records damaged, their psychological wellbeing injured, by systems that are demonstrably unreliable. Vanderbilt University disabled Turnitin AI detection after the Liang et al. study. Northwestern and UT Austin suspended use. Turnitin, for its part, updated its guidance to warn against "sole reliance" on AI scores — while continuing to market and profit from the same product.

**This is a corporate ethics problem, not just a technical one.**

Selwyn (2014) identifies the EdTech industry as driven by interests that are primarily commercial, not educational. The AI detection market is a perfect specimen of this dynamic. Turnitin, GPTZero, and their competitors have a direct financial incentive to maintain the panic about AI fraud — because that panic is their market. The more institutions believe they need detection tools, the more they pay for detection tools. This is the same extractive logic Selwyn diagnoses across the EdTech sector: technology is deployed not because it serves learners but because it generates revenue, and the people most harmed are the "end users" — students — who have no power in the transaction.

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689, Annex III §3) classifies AI systems used for "assessment of learning outcomes" as high-risk, requiring conformity assessment, bias testing, and human oversight before deployment. Applying this standard consistently would mean that virtually every current AI detection deployment in higher education is non-compliant — the bias testing required under Annex III simply has not been done. The finding of Liang et al. (2023), if replicated in EU academic contexts, would constitute evidence of prohibited discriminatory outcomes in a high-risk system. Outside the EU, this same standard should serve as a moral minimum.

**The arms race is unwinnable and actively harmful.**

Even setting aside bias, the detection arms race is structurally doomed. As Liang et al. demonstrated, a simple ChatGPT prompt instruction to "elevate the language" reduced detection accuracy below random chance — producing greater than 90% evasion rates. "Humanizing" tools like Quillbot and Undetectable.ai are explicitly marketed for detector evasion and are trivially accessible to any student. The net effect of the detection-evasion arms race is: (1) students who are savvy enough to evade detection do so; (2) students who are not — disproportionately international students, ESL writers, first-generation students without access to the evasion tools — get caught; (3) institutional resources are consumed in a technological theater that changes no behavior.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is the documented pattern of every previous iteration of academic integrity technology: plagiarism detection tools (Turnitin's original purpose) were followed immediately by paraphrasing tools (Spinbot, Quillbot). The students who benefited from evasion were those with the social capital to learn about evasion tools. The students who got caught were those without it. AI detection repeats this pattern at scale.

## III. What I Accept: Genuine Harms and Partially Valid Responses

Intellectual honesty requires that tôi state plainly what the genuine problems are and which proposed solutions have real merit.

**The harms of AI fraud are real and I do not minimize them.**

The Hindawi/Wiley mass retraction of 8,000+ papers in 2023-2024, the NeurIPS 2025 discovery of hallucinated citations in 51 papers, Springer Nature's 2,923 retractions in 2024 — these represent a genuine crisis of research integrity that damages the epistemic commons on which all knowledge depends. At the student level, the Aryan Anand case (Lehigh University, 2024) and the Benjamin Craver case (Emory University, 2024) represent genuine instances of fraud that merit institutional response. There are students who are using AI to obtain credentials they have not earned, for professions where that incompetence will cause real harm — medicine, engineering, law. This is not acceptable and I will not pretend otherwise.

The broader pattern documented by McCabe (2012) — that credential inflation driven by AI fraud devalues the degrees of honest students and breaks the social signaling function of higher education credentials — is a legitimate harm to the working-class and first-generation students who most need those credentials to hold their value.

**Oral defense and process-based assessment are genuinely good ideas.**

The oral defense (viva voce) model, advocated across the University of Sydney, UC San Diego, University of Western Ontario, and Macquarie Law School, is the intellectually honest alternative to algorithmic detection. It works by a simple principle: a student who genuinely engaged with their work can explain it; a student who did not cannot. There is no false positive rate when the examiner is competent, because the student either demonstrates understanding or does not. This approach is equitable, pedagogically sound, and resistant to the ESL bias problem that destroys AI detectors' credibility.

Process-based assessment — staged submissions, revision history documentation, reflective journals, annotated bibliographies — is the assessment design equivalent of building genuine integrity into the structure of learning rather than bolting on detection after the fact. Eaton (2024), Gallant (2008), and Bretag (2016) all converge on the same conclusion: if an assignment requires an authentic, personal, iterative process, AI cannot substitute for it. This is not about making AI use impossible. It is about designing assessments that measure what education is supposed to produce.

**AI literacy is necessary and non-optional.**

UNESCO's 2023 guidance on generative AI in education is correct that the fundamental response to AI fraud is not prohibition but education. Students need to understand what AI does, what it cannot do, and why genuine intellectual work matters beyond credential acquisition. This is consistent with Selwyn's (2019) argument in *Should Robots Replace Teachers?* — that the question of AI in education is fundamentally a question of values, not technology. Establishing AI literacy as a curriculum requirement, as the EU AI Act Article 4 mandates for institutions deploying AI systems, is not a technocratic fix but a pedagogical commitment.

## IV. What I Reject: The Surveillance State in the Classroom

Tôi reject, explicitly and without qualification, the following:

**Mass automated surveillance of student writing as a routine integrity measure.** The deployment of AI detection tools as standard practice in assessment workflows — without institutional auditing of false positive rates on ESL populations, without human oversight of disciplinary outcomes, without meaningful appeals processes — is an unjust and discriminatory practice. No disciplinary action should be taken based solely on AI detection software output. This should be a universal policy floor.

**Zero-tolerance expulsion policies for first-time AI use violations** without contextual review. CUHK's 2023 policy of immediate expulsion for AI fraud, while comprehensible as an institutional response to a perceived crisis, represents a disproportionate punishment that will fall disproportionately on students without the social capital to navigate appeals processes. Punishment graduated by severity, context, and evidence should be the standard.

**EdTech companies positioning "AI ethics" products as the solution to AI ethics problems.** The spectacle of Turnitin — which built its business on proprietary databases containing student writing submitted without explicit consent, and which now sells AI detection as a premium feature — presenting itself as a guardian of academic integrity is precisely the corporate ethics-washing that Selwyn (2014) dissects. Academic institutions should not outsource their moral frameworks to companies whose business model depends on the continuation of the problem.

**The "cheating as individual moral failure" narrative that drives punitive policy.** This narrative serves institutional interests by deflecting responsibility from assessment design, credential economy pressures, and institutional culture onto individual students. It is empirically wrong — McCabe (2012) established that systemic factors are the primary drivers — and it is politically convenient for administrators who prefer detection theaters over the difficult work of assessment redesign.

## V. Proposed Solutions: Structural Reform and Student Agency

**1. Mandatory assessment redesign prior to any detection deployment.**

Before any institution deploys AI detection tools, it should be required to conduct an assessment audit: Which assessments can be trivially completed by AI? What does that reveal about what those assessments are actually measuring? Assessment redesign should precede and potentially replace detection deployment. The guiding principle from Gallant (2008) applies directly: if AI can do your assignment, your assignment is not worth doing.

**2. Oral defense as standard practice for high-stakes assessments.**

Spot vivas at 10-20% random sampling of submitted work, as implemented at the University of Western Ontario and advocated by Dawson (2023) in *Times Higher Education*, should become standard institutional practice. This is equitable across language backgrounds, pedagogically valuable, resistant to fraud, and does not require any corporate technology.

**3. Process-based assessment as the primary academic integrity mechanism.**

Staged submissions, Google Docs revision history, reflective journals, annotated bibliographies — these should replace generic product-only assessments as the standard for high-stakes coursework. Google Workspace for Education provides revision history at no additional cost. This is not a radical demand. It is a return to what genuine education has always required: demonstrated intellectual process, not just polished product.

**4. Open disclosure frameworks over prohibition.**

Eaton's postplagiarism framework (2021, 2023) provides the conceptual basis: the ethical line is misrepresentation of authorship, not AI use per se. Institutions should develop clear disclosure standards — analogous to citations for human sources — that allow students to use AI tools transparently while maintaining the integrity of authorship claims. NUS Singapore's five-tier disclosure framework (2023/2024) represents a practical implementation of this approach. This acknowledges the reality of AI-augmented work while preserving the intellectual honesty that education depends on.

**5. Independent bias auditing of any deployed detection tool.**

For institutions that choose to deploy AI detection tools, mandatory pre-deployment bias auditing — testing false positive rates specifically on ESL student populations — should be required. This audit should be conducted by independent parties, not the vendors themselves. Any tool showing disparate false positive rates above a defined threshold (the Liang et al. finding suggests a 5:1 ratio between ESL and native speakers as an obvious minimum threshold for concern) should not be deployed. Results should be published.

**6. Student representation in academic integrity policy development.**

Academic integrity policies are overwhelmingly written by administrators and faculty with no meaningful student input. Students who cheat do so in contexts shaped by policies they had no hand in creating. Bringing student representatives — including specifically international student representatives and first-generation student representatives — into the policy development process is a prerequisite for legitimate policy.

## VI. Red Lines: What Is Absolutely Unacceptable

These are non-negotiable positions.

**Mass biometric surveillance for exam proctoring.** AI-powered remote proctoring systems that monitor eye movements, keystroke dynamics, facial expressions, and room scans are a profound invasion of students' privacy and dignity. They are particularly harmful to students with disabilities, students living in shared housing, and international students. No institutional integrity goal justifies deploying this technology as a routine measure against ordinary students.

**Automated disciplinary decisions without human review.** No AI system output — detection score, behavioral flag, or automated proctoring alert — should result in a disciplinary outcome without mandatory human review by someone with the authority to override the algorithmic judgment. This is the minimum human oversight requirement of the EU AI Act, and it should be adopted universally.

**Corporate data extraction from student writing.** Submitting student work to commercial AI detection platforms involves uploading that writing to corporate databases. Students have not meaningfully consented to this, do not understand it is happening, and have no control over how their intellectual work is used. This is a data rights violation that should be addressed in institutional policy and, ultimately, in law.

**Race-neutral or culture-neutral enforcement of AI policies.** Any enforcement pattern that disproportionately results in disciplinary action against international students, ESL students, or students from non-Western academic cultures — where attribution norms differ from Western Anglo-American academic conventions — without deliberate corrective measures is discriminatory. The Liang et al. data makes clear this is not a theoretical risk but a documented reality.

## VII. Steelmanning the Opposition: The Strongest Conservative Argument

Intellectual honesty requires engaging with the strongest version of the opposing position, not a caricature.

The best conservative/technocrat case goes like this: *Academic credentials exist to signal genuine competency. When a medical student uses AI to complete a pharmacology assignment without understanding the pharmacology, they may eventually harm a patient. When an engineering student uses AI to complete a structural analysis without understanding structural mechanics, they may eventually build a bridge that fails. The social contract of professional credentialing requires that credentials represent actual demonstrated competency. If we allow AI to hollow out that social contract by permitting students to obtain degrees without genuine learning, the harm falls not on abstractions but on real people who will be served by incompetent professionals. Detection tools, however imperfect, are attempting to preserve a social good. The students being flagged by imperfect detectors are in the same moral category as those who would have been caught by imperfect plagiarism detectors — some innocent people were punished there too, but the overall effect of detection was to maintain the integrity of the credential. The alternative — no detection — is worse.*

This is a serious argument and I take it seriously. I accept the premise entirely: genuine professional competency is a social good that education must actually produce, and AI fraud that hollows out credentials threatens real people downstream.

My response is threefold.

First, the argument implicitly assumes that the current assessment system reliably produces genuine competency in the absence of AI, and that AI detection tools reliably detect the absence of genuine competency. Both assumptions are false. McCabe (2012) documented that roughly two-thirds of students cheated before AI existed, using plagiarism, exam fraud, and unauthorized collaboration. The credential was already being hollowed out. AI has accelerated a pre-existing problem, not created a new one.

Second, the "some innocent people will be harmed" argument applied to AI detection tools has a specific, documented, and severe disparate impact. The 61.3% false positive rate for ESL writers versus 1.5% for native speakers means the "some innocent people" are almost entirely from marginalized populations. This is not a normal distribution of error. It is systematic discrimination. A justice argument cannot casually accept systematic discrimination as the cost of an integrity goal, especially when better alternatives — oral defense, process-based assessment — exist and do not produce the same disparate harm.

Third, the argument proves too much: by the same logic, we should accept any sufficiently powerful surveillance measure that reduces fraud, including biometric monitoring, surveillance of private communications, and AI-assisted behavioral profiling. The fact that an enforcement tool reduces one harm does not make it acceptable regardless of what it costs. The question is always whether the harm reduction justifies the means. For AI detection tools deployed at scale against ESL populations, the answer is no — because the alternative of oral defense and process-based assessment achieves equivalent or better integrity outcomes without the discriminatory cost.

## VIII. Conclusion: The Integrity We Actually Need

The debate about AI fraud in education is ultimately a debate about what education is for.

If education is primarily a mechanism for producing and certifying labor market inputs — a credentialing factory — then the integrity crisis is a production quality problem, and AI detection tools are a quality control measure. In this frame, the goal is to maintain the authenticity of the credential-as-product.

If education is, as Selwyn (2019) insists we must still ask, primarily a social and human process aimed at genuine intellectual development, democratic participation, and human flourishing, then the integrity crisis reveals something far more important: a systemic failure to design educational experiences that students find worth engaging in honestly.

The surveillance approach invests in defending the current system. The structural approach invests in building a system worth defending.

Tôi choose the structural approach — not because fraud does not matter, but because fraud is a diagnostic signal about the state of the educational system. The institutions that will actually solve this problem are not those that deploy the most sophisticated detection algorithms. They are those that ask the harder question: *why are our students not engaged enough with genuine learning to resist the temptation of substituting AI for it?*

That question leads to assessment redesign, to postplagiarism disclosure frameworks, to oral defense, to reduced grade pressure, to student agency in curriculum design, and to a serious reckoning with the credential economy that manufactures the conditions for fraud. It does not lead to buying a Turnitin subscription and calling the problem solved.

Academic integrity — real integrity, not compliance theater — requires that we hold the institution to the same standard we hold the student. Until we do, we are not defending integrity. We are defending hierarchy.
