# Academic Integrity in the Age of AI: A Free-Market Position Paper

**Framing:** Heritage Foundation / American Enterprise Institute analytical tradition  
**Author role:** Right-wing free-market think tank analyst  
**Topic:** AI fraud and academic integrity in EdTech education  
**Date:** April 2026

---

## Executive Summary

The academic integrity crisis triggered by generative AI is real, documented, and serious. But the policy responses proposed by international bureaucracies — EU AI Act mandates, UNESCO regulatory frameworks, government-imposed surveillance infrastructure — are the wrong cure for a genuine disease. The root cause of AI fraud is not the existence of ChatGPT. It is a university system structurally deformed by credential inflation, perverse incentive architectures, and a bureaucratic monopoly on learning certification. Market mechanisms, reputational accountability, and institutional competition will solve this problem far more effectively — and with fewer collateral harms — than top-down regulatory mandates. This paper makes that case.

---

## 1. Root Cause Diagnosis: The Broken Incentive Architecture

### 1.1 Credential Inflation is the Original Sin

The AI cheating crisis did not begin in November 2022 when ChatGPT launched. It began decades earlier when governments and university systems collectively agreed to treat a four-year degree as the universal entry ticket to the middle class. McCabe, Butterfield, and Treviño's landmark survey of 70,000 US college students (McCabe et al., *Cheating in College*, 2012, Johns Hopkins UP) found that approximately two-thirds of undergraduates self-reported cheating during their academic careers — and this was *before* AI. The Springer 2024 study of 1,386 Vietnamese undergraduates found actual AI fraud rates approximately three times higher than self-reported rates. Neither statistic is surprising once you understand the underlying incentive structure.

When a credential is required for employment regardless of whether the underlying competencies were actually developed, the rational response from a cost-benefit-maximizing student is to acquire the credential at minimum cost. AI simply slashed the marginal cost of credential acquisition dramatically. Fraud exploded because the return on fraud — a diploma — remained high while the cost collapsed. This is not a moral failure of students. It is a predictable market response to a price distortion created by credential inflation, itself a product of government subsidization of universities and employer over-reliance on academic signals.

Gallant (*Academic Integrity in the Twenty-First Century*, ASHE Higher Education Report, 2008) correctly identified that cheating is a "systemic symptom of poorly designed academic environments" — not individual moral failure. We agree with this diagnosis. But the policy prescription that follows from this diagnosis must be structural reform of perverse incentives, not more surveillance and enforcement layered on top of a fundamentally broken system.

### 1.2 The Bureaucratic University Has Designed Assessments That Invite Fraud

If a university designs assessments that AI can trivially complete — five-paragraph essays on topics that have no personal or contextual specificity, generic research papers on well-trodden questions — then AI will complete them. Gallant (2008) states directly: "if AI can trivially complete an assignment, the assignment was not testing a meaningful competency." This is not a complaint about AI. It is a damning indictment of the assessment design competence of universities that have scaled to hundreds of students per course and defaulted to the cheapest possible assessment format.

The Texas A&M case (May 2023) illustrates institutional dysfunction vividly. Instructor Jared Mumm used ChatGPT itself — not a validated detection tool, but the generative model — to evaluate whether students had used AI. ChatGPT falsely claimed authorship of every submission. An entire graduating class faced academic jeopardy because a single instructor lacked basic AI literacy. This is the competence level of the bureaucracy claiming authority to regulate EdTech companies.

### 1.3 The Publish-or-Perish System Has Industrialized Research Fraud

At the research level, the crisis is even more stark. The Hindawi/Wiley mass retraction event (2023) saw 8,000+ papers retracted — approximately 80% of all global retractions that year — with financial losses to Wiley of $35–40 million. Springer Nature retracted 2,923 papers in 2024 alone, a single-publisher annual record. NeurIPS 2025 — the world's premier AI research conference, where papers *about* AI were accepted — was found by GPTZero (January 21, 2026) to contain 100+ hallucinated citations across 51 accepted papers. Every affected paper had cleared peer review by three or more expert reviewers. Detection rate by the peer review system: zero.

These outcomes flow directly from publish-or-perish incentive structures. When researcher promotions, grant access, and institutional prestige rankings depend on publication counts rather than research quality, demand for fraudulent publications is structural and predictable. Paper mills are not aberrations; they are the rational market response to a distorted incentive system that values output quantity over output quality. The Hindawi model — open-access special issues managed by guest editors with minimal oversight — was exactly the kind of institutional corner-cutting that concentrated production pressures create.

## 2. What We Accept from Existing Frameworks

We are not nihilists about academic integrity policy. Several elements of existing frameworks are correct and we endorse them.

### 2.1 Process-Based Assessment: Sound Pedagogy, Not Surveillance

The research consensus around process-based and oral assessment is well-founded. Oral defense (viva voce) — including the "spot viva" model advocated by Dawson (2023, *Times Higher Education*) — authenticates the *person*, not the *product*. A student who genuinely engaged with their work can defend it. One who outsourced it to AI cannot. The University of Western Ontario implemented oral exams for 600 undergraduate business students in 2023–2024; Macquarie Law School has adopted structured viva voce for undergraduate coursework. These are market-tested institutional responses that work.

We accept this recommendation not because it involves more bureaucracy — it requires less — but because it creates the right alignment: students who genuinely learn are rewarded, and students who cheat are exposed through their own demonstrated ignorance. This is assessment design that works *with* market incentives rather than against them.

### 2.2 Eaton's Postplagiarism Framework: A Useful Conceptual Shift

Sarah Elaine Eaton's postplagiarism framework (IJEI, 2023; *Second Handbook of Academic Integrity*, Springer, 2024) correctly identifies that traditional plagiarism definitions are conceptually inadequate for the AI era. The ethical violation is *misrepresentation of authorship*, not AI use per se. A student who discloses AI use and demonstrates genuine understanding has not cheated. A student who submits AI-generated work as their own, without understanding or engaging with it, has defrauded the institution and ultimately themselves.

This reframing is right. It moves from a rule-following compliance model to an outcome-validation model, which is precisely the kind of institutional design thinking we advocate. The question is not "did you use ChatGPT?" but "can you demonstrate that you know this material?" — a question best answered through assessment design, not algorithmic surveillance.

### 2.3 McCabe's Honor Code Research: Culture Outperforms Enforcement

McCabe, Butterfield, and Treviño (2012) demonstrated across 70,000 students that honor code institutions showed approximately 25% lower cheating rates than non-honor code institutions — not because of more punishment, but because of different normative culture. This is powerful evidence that voluntary compliance frameworks built on shared values outperform coercive enforcement. This is the free-market position applied to academic culture: voluntary standards that emerge from institutional reputation competition are more effective than mandated compliance frameworks.

### 2.4 Reputational Pressure on Publishers: Market Accountability Working

The Hindawi/Wiley and Springer Nature retraction crises are being addressed partly through market mechanisms. Impact factors decline when journals publish fraudulent work. Institutional subscriptions are reconsidered when publishers lose credibility. Retraction Watch, a nonprofit, functions as a reputational market-making institution — creating accountability through transparency. Crossref's retraction notification infrastructure is another example of private sector coordination solving a public goods problem without government mandate. These mechanisms should be strengthened, not displaced by regulation.

## 3. What We Reject: Overreaching, Counterproductive, and Harmful Proposals

### 3.1 The EU AI Act Applied to Education: Regulatory Overreach

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classifies AI systems used in "education and vocational training" as high-risk under Annex III where they "determine access to, or important outcomes in education." Article 50 requires disclosure of AI-generated content. This sounds reasonable in principle. In practice, it is:

**Technically premature.** LLM watermarking (Kirchenbauer et al., UMD, ICML 2023; Aaronson, UT Austin/OpenAI) remains undeployed in any production system as of 2025. Kirchenbauer's green/red token watermarking is effective for detection after ~200 tokens at p<0.0001 in laboratory conditions, but is defeated by paraphrase attacks and heavy editing. Mandating disclosure mechanisms for which no reliable technical infrastructure yet exists creates compliance theater, not actual accountability.

**Structurally captured by incumbent players.** Compliance costs for AI Act provisions in education disproportionately burden startups and independent developers. Turnitin — a billion-dollar incumbent — can absorb EU compliance costs. A student developer like Benjamin Craver, who built "Eightball" (an AI study tool) as an undergraduate at Emory and won a $10,000 startup prize, cannot. Emory subsequently suspended him under its honor code for "facilitating" potential AI dishonesty — despite no evidence of actual misuse. The Craver case illustrates the perverse dynamic of institutional over-regulation: the student who builds a legitimate educational tool is punished, while the incumbent surveillance vendor is enriched.

**Creates false precision.** When AI detection tools are mandated and used as dispositive evidence in academic proceedings, false positives become due process crises. Liang et al. (Stanford, 2023, *Patterns*) found that GPTZero and other AI detectors produce false positive rates of 61.3% for TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers. Turnitin's own documentation acknowledges a 4% sentence-level false positive rate. The Texas A&M incident demonstrated that even faculty deployed these tools without understanding their limitations. Mandating their use in high-stakes academic proceedings without robust due process safeguards is institutionalized injustice.

### 3.2 UNESCO Governance Frameworks: Democratic Legitimacy Deficits

UNESCO's "Recommendation on the Ethics of AI" (2021) and "Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research" (2023) carry zero binding authority but enormous normative influence in educational policy circles. The concern is not that these documents contain bad ideas — some of their assessment redesign recommendations are sound — but that they create a compliance culture in which universities in Vietnam, Singapore, and elsewhere rush to demonstrate alignment with international benchmarks set by an unelected international bureaucracy.

Wayne Holmes (UCL, UNESCO consultant) has correctly identified that "unintended consequences" of AI in education require scrutiny. But his solution — more UNESCO-coordinated guidance, more international frameworks, more "community-wide ethics frameworks" — compounds the problem. Each new layer of international governance reduces the space for institutional experimentation and competition that would generate superior solutions through trial and error.

### 3.3 AI Detection as Primary Policy Tool: Surveillance Without Accountability

The UK Office of the Independent Adjudicator (OIA) documented multiple cases (2023–2025) of students falsely accused of AI use based solely on AI detector outputs. UK students — including international ESL students — faced academic sanctions for writing that stylometric analysis incorrectly flagged. This is precisely the false positive problem Liang et al. documented: non-native English writers produce text that AI detectors classify as AI-generated because their prose patterns diverge from native-speaker norms.

Building institutional accountability systems around detection tools with 61% false positive rates on ESL writers is not academic integrity policy. It is institutionalized discrimination against international students. The University of Sheffield's 92 AI misconduct cases in 2023-24 (a 15x increase from the prior year) were detected through a mix of faculty judgment, automated tools, and stylistic inconsistency identification — without any documented false positive audit. The University of Minnesota's 188 AI cases (2023-24) similarly relied on mixed detection methods with no systematic false positive review.

We reject any policy framework that makes AI detector output primary evidence for academic misconduct without independent corroboration, due process protections, and systematic false positive auditing.

### 3.4 EdTech Company Regulation as Integrity Solution: Misidentifying the Problem

Neil Selwyn (*Should Robots Replace Teachers?*, Polity, 2019) argues that EdTech companies are primarily commercial actors whose interests are misaligned with genuine educational outcomes, and that the "billion-dollar EdTech market" of "investors, developers and self-styled edu-preneurs" is disrupting education for profit. Selwyn's sociological critique is sharp: he is right that we should ask "should" rather than merely "can" questions about educational technology.

But Selwyn's prescription — skepticism toward market actors, preference for educator-mediated institutional control — inverts the accountability problem. The entities that have *industrialized* academic fraud are not private EdTech companies. They are:

- Hindawi (a Wiley subsidiary), an open-access publisher operating under "publish or perish" market incentives created by academic prestige rankings
- State university systems that scaled to hundreds of students per course and defaulted to AI-completable generic essay assessments
- The peer review system at NeurIPS — the world's premier AI research conference — that achieved zero detection rate on 100+ hallucinated citations in 51 papers

By contrast, Khan Academy's Khanmigo (a private EdTech product) is designed on Socratic principles that *refuse* to complete student work, instead asking questions that require the student to demonstrate understanding. Coursera's AI-assisted viva model generates follow-up questions from submitted work to verify student comprehension. Duolingo's proctored exam infrastructure maintains assessment integrity at scale. These are private market solutions outperforming university bureaucracies on exactly the metrics Selwyn values.

## 4. Our Proposed Solutions: Market Mechanisms, Competition, and Credential Reform

### 4.1 Competitive Credential Unbundling

The deepest structural fix is breaking the monopoly of the four-year degree credential. When employers can hire on the basis of verified competency demonstrations — project portfolios, credentialed micro-qualifications, work-sample tests, apprenticeship records — rather than degree signals alone, the return on credential fraud collapses. The degree becomes one signal among many rather than the universal entry ticket. Google, Apple, and IBM's Skills First hiring initiatives are moving in this direction. Government policy should facilitate, not obstruct, this transition by removing regulatory preferences for degree-holder hiring in public procurement and professional licensing where competency can be directly verified.

### 4.2 Reputation-Based Institutional Accountability

Institutions that systematically produce graduates without competencies should face market consequences. This requires:

- **Transparent graduate employment outcome data** reported at the program level (not just institution level), enabling prospective students and employers to evaluate institutional quality on real outcomes
- **Retraction-adjusted impact factors** for academic publishing — journal reputation systems should penalize systematic fraud, as markets already do imperfectly through impact factor dynamics
- **Third-party competency verification** — employers and graduate programs that independently test applicant competencies create market pressure on institutions to ensure their graduates can actually perform

Rose Luckin (*Machine Learning and Human Intelligence*, UCL IOE Press, 2018) argues persuasively that our "impoverished evaluation of human intelligence" — over-reliance on IQ tests, standardized scores, and credential signals — causes us to "undervalue the most valuable resource in the world: ourselves." Luckin is right. The solution is richer, more granular competency demonstration — which market-based portfolio systems provide better than state-mandated standardized assessment.

### 4.3 Private Sector Attribution Standards

Rather than EU AI Act mandated disclosure mechanisms dependent on undeployed watermarking technology, we advocate for voluntary industry attribution standards developed by AI companies, publishers, and academic institutions through private coordination.

The Hindawi case demonstrates the value of private reputational infrastructure: Retraction Watch (a nonprofit), Crossref (a nonprofit industry consortium), and iThenticate (a private company) collectively provide more effective accountability infrastructure than any government mandate. The model is private standard-setting (similar to how IEEE and ACM codes of ethics function) rather than legislative mandate.

If OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google were to voluntarily implement LLM watermarking — and there is meaningful commercial incentive to do so, since provenance verification serves their business interests in litigation and fraud contexts — the attribution problem becomes technically tractable. Kirchenbauer's UMD watermarking approach is technically sound; the barrier is not capability but deployment incentives. Creating liability frameworks that make AI companies partially responsible for fraudulent academic uses of their products would internalize this externality without requiring the surveillance architecture of the EU AI Act.

### 4.4 Assessment Market Competition

Allow institutions to differentiate on assessment quality. Universities that implement rigorous oral defense, portfolio-based, and process-documented assessment will produce graduates whose competencies are verifiably demonstrated. In a competitive market for credentials, these institutions' graduates will command employment premiums, creating positive feedback that drives assessment quality upward across the sector.

The University of Western Ontario's oral exam implementation for 600 undergraduate business students (2023-2024), Macquarie Law School's viva voce program, and University of Sydney's Learning Futures process portfolio framework are not government mandates — they are institutional competitive differentiators. Policy should create the market conditions under which these innovations spread through competitive imitation, not mandate their adoption through UNESCO compliance requirements.

## 5. Red Lines: What Is Absolutely Unacceptable

### 5.1 AI Detector Output as Sole Evidence in Academic Proceedings

Given the documented 61.3% false positive rate for ESL writers (Liang et al., Stanford, 2023), any academic proceeding that results in grade penalty, suspension, or expulsion based primarily on AI detector output — without independent corroboration — is a due process violation. We draw this as an absolute line: AI detection scores are probabilistic evidence requiring human judgment and independent verification, not dispositive proof.

### 5.2 Mandatory Surveillance of Student Writing Processes

The EU AI Act's high-risk classification of educational AI systems, if extended to require continuous monitoring of student writing processes (keylogging, screen recording, revision history analysis), would create a surveillance environment incompatible with academic freedom and student privacy rights. Process documentation (Google Docs revision history) is valuable as a *voluntary* institutional tool; it becomes unacceptable when mandated under threat of automatic suspicion.

### 5.3 Penalizing EdTech Innovation Without Evidence of Harm

The Craver/Emory case (2024) — where a student who won a university startup competition for an AI study tool was subsequently suspended under the honor code for "facilitating" potential misuse — is the paradigmatic policy failure we oppose. Punishing tool-builders for theoretical downstream misuse without evidence of actual harm chills legitimate EdTech innovation, discriminates against students with entrepreneurial skills, and protects incumbent vendors from competitive challenge.

### 5.4 Any Policy That Creates Discriminatory False Positive Risk

Given documented ESL false positive rates, any integrity policy framework that fails to include explicit false positive auditing by student demographic (international vs. domestic, ESL vs. native speakers) is structurally discriminatory. We oppose CUHK's 2023 blanket AI prohibition policy not on substantive grounds but on procedural grounds: a prohibition enforced through unreliable detection creates disproportionate enforcement risk for international students.

## 6. Steelmanning the Opposition: The Strongest Left-Wing Counter-Argument

The strongest case against our position is not Selwyn's sociological critique of EdTech companies, which is partially correct but misidentifies the primary institutional failure. The strongest case is this:

**Market mechanisms and reputation competition are necessary but insufficient because the victims of academic fraud externalities — future employers, patients treated by incompetent medical graduates, citizens governed by law school fraudsters — have no market power in the educational credential transaction.** The student and the university negotiate the credential; the downstream victim has no voice in that negotiation. This is a genuine market failure: credential fraud is an externality borne by third parties with no ability to contract for protection.

This argument justifies *some* regulatory intervention in academic integrity — specifically in high-stakes professional licensing contexts (medicine, law, engineering) where credential fraud has direct public safety implications. The Neurosurgical Review flooding (129+ AI-generated papers retracted, Springer Nature 2024-2025) is a genuine patient safety risk that the journal's market incentives did not prevent quickly enough.

**Our response:** We accept this argument in professional licensing contexts and support mandatory competency examinations for professional entry that are *independent of* undergraduate credentials — bar exams, medical licensing boards, engineering professional examinations. These are market-supplementing interventions, not market-replacing ones. They do not require building a surveillance apparatus inside universities; they require building robust independent verification at the point where credential claims meet professional practice.

For non-professional general education, the externality argument is weaker. The primary victim of an English literature student's AI-assisted essay fraud is the student themselves, who fails to develop genuine analytical capacity. Market mechanisms — employer competency testing, portfolio verification, competitive reputation systems — are adequate to address this harm without regulatory mandates.

The left-wing critique of Luckin's optimistic AI-augmentation framing is also worth steelmanning. Luckin (*Machine Learning and Human Intelligence*, 2018) argues that AI can be a democratic tool that enriches human intelligence and expands educational access. Selwyn counters that this vision underweights the commercial imperatives of EdTech companies and the structural power asymmetries in educational AI deployment. Both are partially right. The question is not whether AI in education *can* augment human intelligence but whether the specific institutional arrangements under which it is deployed create incentives for augmentation or substitution. Our answer: the institution determines the outcome, and the policy lever is institutional competition and design, not AI regulation.

## Conclusion: Align Incentives, Trust Markets, Preserve Due Process

The academic integrity crisis is real. The scale is documented: 8,000+ Hindawi retractions, 2,923 Springer Nature retractions in a single year, 100+ NeurIPS hallucinated citations cleared by expert peer review, 188 University of Minnesota students found responsible for AI violations in a single academic year, a 15x increase in AI misconduct cases at University of Sheffield in a single year. These numbers reflect a genuine structural failure.

But the structural failure precedes AI. AI has exposed and accelerated a credential system that was already deformed by inflation, perverse publish-or-perish incentives, and bureaucratic assessment design that invited fraud. Layering EU AI Act mandates, UNESCO governance frameworks, and institutional surveillance infrastructure on top of these structural failures will produce compliance theater and discriminatory enforcement — not genuine integrity.

The right policy program is:

1. Break the monopoly of the four-year degree through credential unbundling and employer competency verification
2. Build transparent, market-facing institutional accountability through public outcome data and reputational infrastructure
3. Pursue voluntary private sector attribution standards rather than regulatory mandates dependent on undeployed technology
4. Allow and incentivize institutional competition on assessment quality, spreading oral defense and process portfolio models through market selection
5. Maintain absolute due process protections against AI detector false positives, especially for ESL and international students
6. Reserve mandatory intervention for professional licensing contexts where credential fraud creates direct public safety externalities

Eaton is right that "we can outsource control of writing to AI but cannot outsource responsibility." Luckin is right that AI can enrich human intelligence if institutions are designed to make it a tutor rather than a substitute. McCabe is right that culture outperforms enforcement. The market-oriented synthesis of these insights is: design institutions that align individual incentives with genuine learning, hold those institutions accountable through transparent reputation systems, and trust students, educators, and companies to navigate the result without a surveillance state watching every keystroke.
