# Proportionate Integrity: Why High-Stakes Professional Certification Requires Stronger Tools Than Mass Undergraduate Assessment

**Position Paper — Professional Licensing Board / Public Safety Ethics Perspective**

*Grounded in: Proportionality doctrine in administrative law; public safety ethics (Beauchamp & Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, 8th ed.); professional competency literature (NBME, FSMB, NCSBN); Vietnam Luật Khám bệnh chữa bệnh 2023; USMLE security research; risk-calibrated governance theory (Hood, 1986; Baldwin, Cave & Lodge, 2012).*

---

## Framing: The Intellectual Error the Debate Made

This debate has been largely productive. The consensus that mass undergraduate surveillance is wrong, that AI detection tools are unreliable as sole evidence, and that assessment redesign is the correct structural response to the AI fraud problem — all of this is defensible and largely correct.

But the debate made one error that is significant enough to require a dedicated response: it treated "surveillance" and "proctoring" as monolithic categories and rejected them wholesale. In doing so, it conflated two fundamentally different contexts — mass undergraduate surveillance of ordinary coursework, and targeted professional certification monitoring for high-stakes licensing — and applied arguments valid for the first to dismiss the second without examination.

The meta-review of this debate noted the problem directly: "The universal rejection conflates mass undergraduate surveillance with targeted professional certification monitoring." This position paper makes that distinction explicit, argues that it is not merely an academic quibble but a morally load-bearing distinction, and proposes a proportionality framework that the debate failed to articulate.

To be precise about what tôi am and am not arguing: tôi accept without reservation that mass AI detection tools deployed against undergraduate student writing are unjust, technically unreliable, and disproportionately harmful to vulnerable students. That case has been made compellingly and tôi will not contest it. What tôi contest is the inferential step from "mass undergraduate surveillance is wrong" to "therefore all monitoring in all certification contexts is wrong." That step is logically invalid, and the cost of making it is that Vietnam's emerging professional licensing infrastructure — particularly the medical licensing regime taking effect in 2027 — is left without a defensible integrity framework.

---

## I. The Proportionality Principle Is Already Embedded in Our Institutions

Every participant in this debate implicitly accepts the proportionality principle when applied to non-educational contexts. Consider what we already accept as legitimate:

**Airport security screening**: Passengers submit to intrusive physical search, biometric identity verification, and bag scanning. This is accepted not because travel is inherently suspicious but because the consequences of catastrophic failure (aircraft terrorism) are severe enough to justify the privacy cost. No one in this debate argues that airport security violates human dignity and should be abolished.

**Drug testing for pilots and surgeons**: Commercial pilots and operating surgeons in most jurisdictions are subject to mandatory chemical testing. This is accepted because the consequences of impairment in these roles — mass casualties, patient death — justify monitoring that would be entirely inappropriate in a marketing firm.

**Identity verification in banking**: Every financial transaction above a threshold triggers verification protocols, record-keeping, and sometimes real-time monitoring. This is accepted because the fraud stakes justify the friction and privacy cost.

The question, then, is never "is monitoring acceptable in principle?" The question is always: *at what stakes level is monitoring proportionate?* This is the core question the debate failed to ask, because it was so focused on correctly rejecting undergraduate surveillance that it never looked up to ask where the threshold lies.

Professional licensing — medicine, law, engineering, nursing, teaching — meets this threshold. The argument that follows is not ideological. It is proportionality arithmetic: the consequences of fraudulent credentialing in these professions are qualitatively different from those of a fraudulently submitted marketing essay, and the integrity mechanisms must reflect that difference.

## II. The Stakes Calculus in Professional Licensing Is Categorically Different

The Left-Wing paper's strongest argument is this: when Turnitin falsely flags an international student's essay as AI-generated, a human being suffers — academic suspension, lost visa status, psychological harm. This is a real harm and it matters. Tôi accept it entirely.

But consider the obverse: when a candidate passes a medical licensing examination using AI-generated answers to clinical pharmacology questions, without actually understanding those concepts, and then receives a license to prescribe medications to patients — a different human being suffers. A patient suffers. And unlike the falsely accused student, the patient does not know they are at risk.

This asymmetry is what makes the professional licensing context different. In undergraduate assessment:

- The person who bears the cost of a false positive is the student accused
- The person who bears the cost of a false negative (undetected fraud) is primarily the student who benefits from an unearned credential, with diffuse downstream signaling harms

In professional licensing:

- The person who bears the cost of a false positive (wrongly blocked from a profession) is the candidate — a serious harm deserving procedural protection
- The person who bears the cost of a false negative (fraudulent license granted) is a patient, a client, a structural occupant, or an air passenger — a third party who had no agency in the exam process and no knowledge of the risk

The moment fraud harms land on third parties with no ability to protect themselves, the moral calculus changes. This is not a novel insight — it is the foundational logic behind why we regulate medicine, law, engineering, and aviation with licensing requirements in the first place. The licensing examination is the gate. Its integrity is what makes the license meaningful as a public safety instrument.

**Vietnam's specific context makes this concrete.** The Luật Khám bệnh, chữa bệnh 2023 (Law on Medical Examination and Treatment, effective 2024) establishes a national medical licensing examination system, with doctors beginning licensed practice under the new regime starting January 1, 2027, followed by nurses and midwives in 2028, and other health professions in 2029. Vietnam is building this system from scratch. The integrity architecture it adopts in 2027 will set the template for a generation of medical practice. If that architecture is inadequate — if it fails to prevent candidates from using AI-generated knowledge to pass licensing examinations they could not pass on their own competence — patients will pay the price in the clinics and hospitals of 2030 and beyond.

## III. What "Targeted Proctoring" Actually Means — Not the Strawman

The debate's rejection of proctoring was aimed primarily at two targets: AI-powered remote proctoring systems that monitor eye movements, keystroke dynamics, and room scans of undergraduate students sitting at home; and AI detection tools applied to submitted written work. Both are legitimate targets.

But the professional licensing advocate is not proposing either of these things. The USMLE model — which has operated without significant fraud incidents for decades — looks like this:

**In-person testing at authorized centers**: Candidates present to a Prometric or designated testing facility. The in-person setting eliminates the surveillance-capitalism concerns about webcam monitoring of home environments. There is no room scan, no keystroke analysis, no eye-movement tracking.

**Identity verification**: Photo ID matched against registration. Human verification by a proctor. This is the same process used at every bank, airport, and government office in the world.

**Secure exam environment**: No external devices, standardized workstation, supervised testing room. Again — this is what every bar exam, every professional certification exam, every CPA exam has always required.

**Video recording for audit purposes**: Not behavioral analysis. Audit. If a complaint is later filed alleging impersonation or material assistance, the recording provides evidence. This is analogous to the security camera in a bank.

**Post-exam anomaly review**: Statistical flagging of improbably similar answer patterns among co-scheduled test-takers — a method that detects coordinated fraud (impersonation rings, answer-sharing schemes) rather than individual AI use, and which requires human expert review before any action.

**Specific to AI concerns — oral clinical assessment**: Medical licensing worldwide includes clinical skills examinations (USMLE Step 2 CS, OSCE formats) that cannot be delegated to AI because they require real-time interaction with standardized patients. A candidate who memorized AI-generated pharmacology answers will still struggle in a clinical encounter with a human evaluator. The integration of secure knowledge examination with human-evaluated clinical skills is precisely the multi-layer system that professional licensing already uses.

None of this is Proctorio. None of it is ExamSoft's remote webcam behavioral monitoring deployed against undergraduates in their bedrooms. The debate's rejection of the latter does not logically entail rejection of the former, but it was allowed to do so without challenge.

## IV. The Debate's Core Inferential Error

The Left-Wing paper correctly demolished two targets:

1. Mass AI detection tools (Turnitin, GPTZero) deployed as automated disciplinary evidence against undergraduate writers — demolished on the grounds of the 61.3% false positive rate against ESL writers documented by Liang et al. (2023). Correct. These tools should not be used as primary disciplinary evidence.

2. CUHK's zero-tolerance expulsion policy for AI use in undergraduate coursework — criticized as disproportionate. The criticism has merit.

From these correct conclusions, the debate silently imported a third conclusion that does not follow from them: *therefore, monitoring mechanisms are unjustified in all assessment contexts.*

This inference collapses three distinct things into one:

**Detection** (inferring past fraud from text analysis of submitted work) — unreliable, biased, correctly rejected.

**Remote behavioral proctoring of undergraduates at home** (Proctorio, ExamSoft consumer product) — intrusive, inadequately validated, inappropriate for routine undergraduate use. Correctly rejected.

**In-person controlled examination with human proctors for professional licensing** (USMLE, bar exam, NCLEX-RN format) — standard international practice, decades of operating history, no significant fraud incidents, and entirely different in character from either of the above.

The Educator paper's failure to distinguish assessment type from certification type was particularly consequential. Assessment redesign — process portfolios, oral defenses, staged submissions — is the correct approach for undergraduate coursework where the goal is learning. It is not a complete substitute for secure gatekeeping in professional licensing where the goal is verified competency at a specific moment, for a specific legal purpose, affecting third parties.

The OSCE analogy is useful: no one argues that the OSCE (Objective Structured Clinical Examination), in which a medical student is directly observed by an examiner while performing clinical procedures on a standardized patient, constitutes inappropriate surveillance. It is understood as a necessary competency verification mechanism. Secure knowledge examination is the cognitive-domain equivalent of the OSCE's procedural-domain verification.

## V. The Consent Argument: Professional Licensing Candidates Are Not Undergraduate Students

The Left-Wing paper's power comes partly from identifying an acute power imbalance: undergraduate students in required courses, often under financial pressure, often international, often from non-Western academic cultures, subjected to automated systems they cannot challenge.

The professional licensing candidate is in a structurally different position in several relevant ways:

**Informed voluntary entry**: A candidate for the Vietnamese national medical licensing examination has completed six to seven years of medical education. They know they will sit a licensing examination. They know that examination will be proctored. They have had years to understand the terms of entry into the profession. The power imbalance is not zero, but it is categorically different from the undergraduate required to submit an essay through Turnitin without meaningful explanation of what happens to their data.

**The choice to proceed is genuine**: The Left-Wing correctly notes that refusing an exam is not always a realistic choice for economically vulnerable students. But the timeline is different in licensing. Candidates who become aware of proctoring requirements have years before they sit the exam, not days. They are adults who have made a career commitment.

**Due process protections can and should be robust**: The professional licensing context is fully compatible with the due process framework articulated in the Due Process position. If a candidate is flagged for anomaly, they must receive: written notice of the specific concern, opportunity to respond with evidence, independent expert review, and a decision by a human body — not an algorithm. These protections are what distinguishes legitimate gatekeeping from arbitrary authority, and tôi insist on them as non-negotiable components of the framework, not afterthoughts.

**Candidate consent to security conditions**: Every USMLE candidate signs an agreement that explicitly describes the security conditions of the examination. Turnitin is deployed against undergraduates who, in most institutions, have not meaningfully consented to commercial data extraction. These are not equivalent situations.

## VI. Proposed Proportionality Framework

The debate produced a binary: surveillance or no surveillance. The correct framework is tiered proportionality, calibrated to stakes and third-party impact:

| Tier | Context | Stakes | Third-party impact | Appropriate mechanisms |
|------|---------|--------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **Tier 1** | Undergraduate coursework (essays, projects, routine assignments) | Low-medium | Diffuse, indirect | Assessment redesign; clear AI use policy; disclosure framework; NO automated detection as primary evidence |
| **Tier 2** | High-stakes undergraduate (final exams, dissertation, professional practice preparation units) | Medium-high | Indirect | In-person proctored exam OR supervised oral defense; process portfolio; no remote behavioral surveillance |
| **Tier 3** | Professional licensing (medicine, law, engineering, teaching, nursing) | Very high | Direct — patients, clients, structural users, students | Secure in-person examination at authorized centers; human identity verification; video audit capability; multi-modal assessment including direct clinical/practical evaluation; robust due process for any anomaly flagging |

This is not a novel framework — it is a description of what every functional professional licensing system already does. The contribution is making explicit why the Tier 1 critique does not apply to Tier 3, and resisting the debate's tendency to collapse all three tiers into a single rejection.

**For Vietnam specifically, the Tier 3 implementation recommendations are:**

1. Medical licensing (2027): In-person examination at designated centers (not remote), human proctors with standardized training, identity verification by photo ID, video recording for audit (not behavioral analysis), clinical skills component (OSCE format) conducted by licensed physician examiners. No AI detection tools as evidence of knowledge-exam fraud — anomaly review by statistical methods with mandatory human expert review before any action.

2. Legal licensing (Kỳ thi luật sư): Same in-person secure exam model. No home-based remote proctoring. Oral advocacy assessment component conducted by Bar Association evaluators — which cannot be delegated to AI and tests the practical reasoning that written knowledge exams leave underspecified.

3. Teaching certification: Practical teaching evaluation by trained evaluators — this is where authentic competency lives, and it is already fraud-resistant because it requires real-time human performance in front of a class.

4. Engineering licensing: Professional competency examination at authorized centers; multi-stage design problem assessment under supervision.

## VII. Where This Position Concedes — and Why It Still Matters

Intellectual integrity requires tôi to state what this position does not defend.

**Tôi do not defend:**
- Turnitin AI Detection, GPTZero, or any probabilistic text analysis tool as primary evidence in disciplinary proceedings at any tier
- Remote behavioral proctoring (Proctorio, ExamSoft consumer product) in undergraduate home-exam contexts
- Automated disciplinary decisions without mandatory human review at any tier
- Zero-tolerance policies without contextual review and graduated response
- Biometric surveillance beyond basic identity verification (photo ID comparison)
- Extension of professional licensing proctoring logic to any non-licensing context

**Tôi accept:**
- The Liang et al. (2023) finding is devastating for detection-as-evidence at Tier 1. It is not relevant to Tier 3 in-person proctoring, where the mechanism is prevention not detection, and where false positive risk from text analysis bias does not apply.
- The Left-Wing critique of surveillance capitalism in undergraduate EdTech is largely correct and important.
- Assessment redesign is the dominant correct response for Tier 1 and should be the primary institutional investment.
- The Economist's NPV calculation for spot vivas is right for Tier 1 and Tier 2. It is incomplete for Tier 3, where the downstream cost of fraudulent credentialing includes patient safety and professional liability orders of magnitude larger than the cost of examination security.

**Why this still matters:**

Because Vietnam is building its medical licensing system right now. The policy architecture adopted in the next two to three years will determine whether the 2027 licensing examination is a credible gatekeeping mechanism or a credential-dispenser that gives patients a false sense of security. The debate's correct rejection of undergraduate surveillance should not be allowed to silently contaminate the professional licensing design process by creating a presumption that any examination security is illegitimate.

The Educator paper's "assessment redesign solves everything" claim, if imported wholesale into the medical licensing context, produces an outcome that no one in the debate actually wants: a licensing system that relies entirely on portfolio self-report and oral defense without secure knowledge verification, in a context where the stakes are patient lives.

## VIII. Responding to the Steelman of the Opposition

The strongest counter-argument is this: once we accept the proportionality principle for professional licensing, where does it stop? By the same logic, employers could justify surveillance of high-stakes employees; immigration authorities could justify monitoring of visa-holders; landlords could justify monitoring of high-risk tenants. The principle, once accepted, is expansive.

This is a serious argument. Tôi take it seriously because slippage is real — institutions do extend surveillance frameworks beyond their original justifications.

The limiting principle is: **third-party safety impact from an institutionally-verified gatekeeping event.** Professional licensing examinations are a specific, narrow category: they are legal instruments, they produce a status with regulatory consequences, they affect the safety of identifiable third parties who have no agency in the credentialing process, and they are voluntarily entered by candidates who have prior notice of conditions. This combination of factors — legal status, regulatory consequence, third-party safety, candidate notice — does not generalize easily to employee monitoring, immigration surveillance, or residential monitoring.

The airline pilot drug-testing analogy holds: we do not apply mandatory random drug testing to office workers, but we do apply it to pilots, because pilots' impairment has direct consequences for passengers who cannot detect the pilot's state. Professional licensing operates under the same logic. The analogy does not extend to contexts where the third-party safety nexus is absent or speculative.

---

## IX. Conclusion: The Stakes Justify the Scrutiny We Already Apply

Every working physician in Vietnam completed a licensing examination. Every licensed attorney passed a bar exam. Every registered nurse sat a board certification. These examinations are not perfect instruments — no assessment is. But they serve a real function: they are the moment at which the public's interest in not being treated by incompetent practitioners is directly asserted, with legal force.

When AI makes it possible to pass a knowledge examination without the knowledge it is supposed to certify, the public's interest is directly threatened. The response is not to remove the examination. The response is to ensure the examination is conducted under conditions that preserve its function as a genuine gatekeeping mechanism.

The debate was correct that mass undergraduate surveillance is wrong. Automated AI detection tools are unreliable and should not be used as primary disciplinary evidence. Assessment redesign is the dominant correct response for undergraduate education.

But the debate erred in allowing those correct conclusions to foreclose the professional licensing question. The licensing examination is a different instrument, serving a different function, with different stakes, different consent conditions, and direct patient-safety consequences. The proportionality principle that rightly condemns surveillance of a student writing a marketing essay also rightly requires something more from a licensing examination that will authorize the holder to prescribe medications, represent clients in court, sign off on structural engineering calculations, or enter a classroom as a certified educator.

Vietnam's medical licensing system, taking effect in 2027, deserves a security framework proportionate to what is at stake: not the mass surveillance apparatus that this debate correctly rejected, but the targeted, human-supervised, in-person examination infrastructure that professional licensing systems worldwide have operated successfully for decades. Building that infrastructure now — with robust due process protections, human review at every decision point, and clear legal standards for evidence — is the work that this debate's consensus, powerful as it is on the undergraduate question, leaves unfinished.

Academic integrity debates that stop at the university course and do not look forward to the clinic, the courtroom, or the construction site have not finished the argument. This position paper insists that they should.
